REGIONS · LANDSCAPES

Houze Prairie

An expansive, semi-arid savanna and grassland ecosystem found on Ni'Khar, both the Known World and beyond. The ecosystem is defined by fast-growing, flammable, and aggressive Houze grass, which can make up 90% or more of local plant life and crowd out competitors.

01 · INTRODUCTION

Overview

The Houze Prairie is an extensive tropical grassland ecosystem distributed across much of Ni'Khar. It is among the most distinctive and ecologically consequential terrestrial habitats of the Known World, not merely because of its immense geographical extent, but because its modern character is the product of a comparatively recent and unusually disruptive biological invasion. The habitat is defined by the dominance of houze grass, a drought-tolerant, fire-adapted grass whose unusual reproductive and physiological adaptations have allowed it to transform vast areas of tropical prairie, savanna, and even semi-desert into a landscape dominated by its own growth. Although houze grass is not intrinsically superior to many of Kaimere's other grasses, its capacity to propagate fire gives it an ecological advantage that has enabled it to overcome competitors and establish near-monodominant stands across otherwise dissimilar environments.

The Houze Prairie is frequently compared to the African savannas of Earth, particularly those in which extensive grasslands, scattered trees, and large migratory herbivores coexist under a strongly seasonal tropical climate. The comparison is useful, although incomplete. The Houze Prairie encompasses a broader range of environmental conditions than a conventional savanna, including open grassland, semi-arid steppe, seasonal floodplain, and wooded grassland. Some regions are nearly treeless and support dense, continuous stands of houze grass, whereas others retain scattered trees, gallery forests, marshes, and a more diverse assemblage of grasses and shrubs. Its defining characteristic is therefore not a uniform vegetation structure, but the overwhelming ecological influence of houze grass across a geographically extensive and climatically variable landscape.

The modern habitat developed through a combination of biological invasion, climatic suitability, and disturbance-driven ecological change. Houze grass originated in Kairul, where it had already become a dominant component of tropical grasslands several million years ago. Its spread beyond its original range was facilitated by the movement of Kairulan grazing animals, which carried its seeds into new environments and were already physiologically adapted to consuming it. The grass subsequently expanded into the grasslands of Ni'Khar, where its competitive effects were amplified by the relative absence of organisms adapted to its chemical defenses and low nutritional value. Its establishment was not simply a gradual replacement of one grass species by another. It initiated a widespread ecological turnover in which repeated fires, altered ground conditions, and selective pressures on herbivores contributed to the collapse of many pre-existing plant and animal communities.

The transformation was particularly severe in southern Ni'Khar. Before the arrival of houze grass, its prairies supported a substantially more diverse assemblage of grasses, trees, and herbaceous plants. The landscape consisted of mixed grassland and woodland communities rather than the extensive near-monocultures characteristic of much of the modern prairie. Within what may have been only several thousand years, houze grass became dominant across much of this territory. The resulting floral turnover eliminated or severely reduced hundreds of plant species and altered the food resources available to the resident herbivorous fauna. The consequences extended beyond the vegetation itself. Native herbivores that had evolved within the former mixed-grassland system found the new dominant forage difficult to digest, nutritionally inadequate, or chemically unsuitable. Several large-bodied grazers, including proboscideans, megathere sloths, camels, rhinoceroses, pronghorns, and herbivorous dinosaurs, declined severely or disappeared from the affected regions. This in turn caused the turnover of numerous predators that depended on these herbivores like borophagines, megaraptors, and machairodonts.

The resulting ecosystem was consequently not a simple expansion of an existing Kairulan grassland into Ni'Khar. It was a new ecological system established through the displacement of an older one. Kairulan herbivores possessed a substantial advantage because they had evolved alongside houze grass and were already capable of exploiting its unusual nutritional and chemical properties. Their movement into the Known World helped reinforce the grass's dominance, creating a close ecological relationship between the plant and a grazing community adapted to its exploitation. The interaction between these organisms became one of the principal mechanisms maintaining the modern prairie.

The spread of houze grass was not unlimited. It is poorly suited to persist in deeply shaded environments, saturated soils, and regions where vigorous competing vegetation can prevent its establishment. The Seridic Wetlands and their tributaries, as well as the northern rainforests, have remained important barriers to its expansion. These areas retain environmental conditions that houze grass cannot readily exploit, particularly persistent moisture, dense vegetation, and competition from established plant communities. Consequently, the modern prairie is bordered and interrupted by ecological refuges that preserve a substantially different vegetation structure from the surrounding grasslands.

The Houze Prairie is therefore best understood as a disturbance-maintained tropical grassland complex whose distribution depends upon the interaction of seasonal moisture limitation, recurrent fire, grazing pressure, and regional topography. Its apparent uniformity across large areas conceals considerable geographical variation. The northern margins grade into arid steppe and semi-desert, the central region contains the extensive grassland formations most characteristic of the habitat, and the southern margins become progressively more wooded and hydrologically complex. These variations produce a wide range of local environments while retaining the dominant ecological influence of houze grass.

02 · Landscape of the Prairie

Geography and Habitat Structure

The Houze Prairie occupies an extensive but geographically discontinuous region extending from the tropical grasslands of Kairul into Ni'Khar. Within Ni'Khar, its distribution is particularly extensive across the southern and central portions of the continent, where it occupies broad interior lowlands and open terrain between the major western mountain system and the Silverback Mountain range. Its northernmost extensions approach the drier steppe and semi-desert environments of northern Ni'Khar, while its southern margins gradually become more wooded and increasingly influenced by seasonal wetlands. The prairie is therefore not bounded by a single climatic or geological feature. Instead, its distribution reflects the combined influence of rainfall, elevation, drainage, vegetation competition, and the availability of suitable conditions for recurrent fire.

The geographical structure of Ni'Khar is particularly important to the arrangement of the prairie. The continent's extensive mountain systems divide its interior into distinct climatic and ecological regions, creating substantial differences in precipitation between exposed slopes, sheltered basins, and lower-lying corridors. These mountains also interrupt the otherwise relatively continuous distribution of open habitats, producing localized transitions between grassland, woodland, and montane vegetation. Much of the Houze Prairie occupies the lower terrain between these elevated regions, where seasonal rainfall and relatively open topography permit the development of extensive grassland.

The central portion of the Ni'Khari prairie constitutes its principal ecological core. Here, the landscape is characterized by extensive stands of houze grass, generally reaching waist or chest height under favorable growing conditions. Its appearance is dominated by the characteristic orange and yellow coloration of the grass, producing a distinctive landscape that differs considerably from the greener and more structurally diverse prairies that preceded its arrival. Although houze grass can exceed two meters in height under particularly favorable conditions, its stature varies considerably according to rainfall, soil conditions, grazing pressure, and the interval since the most recent fire.

The central prairie is not entirely devoid of woody vegetation. Trees are concentrated around watercourses, seasonal floodplains, and other locations where soil moisture remains available for longer periods. These gallery forests provide localized areas of shade and structural complexity within the otherwise open grassland. Their distribution is particularly important because the surrounding grassland is comparatively unsuitable for many forest-adapted plants. The persistence of trees is therefore strongly associated with local hydrological conditions, which can provide some protection against the seasonal drying and recurrent fires characteristic of the broader prairie.

The houze maple is the most conspicuous tree associated with the modern grassland. Despite its common name, it is a species of ginkgo rather than a true maple. Its rapid growth, low water requirements, and exceptionally fire-resistant tissues allow it to persist in environments where many other trees cannot establish themselves. Houze maples are particularly abundant across the prairie and account for approximately ninety percent of its tree population in some regions. Their distinctive yellow and orange foliage contributes to the characteristic coloration of the landscape throughout the year. Although they are not especially competitive in humid environments or dense forests, they are well adapted to the open, fire-prone conditions of the Houze Prairie.

The distribution of houze maple is not uniform. Trees are often concentrated around localized areas of favorable soil moisture, drainage, and protection, while extensive tracts of open grassland may contain very few. Their abundance nevertheless makes them an important structural component of the habitat. In addition to providing shade and localized shelter, their exceptionally productive seed crops create concentrated food resources within an otherwise nutritionally poor grassland. Other trees, including acacias, figs, and olives introduced during the North African harvest, occur in notable but comparatively limited numbers. These species are substantially outnumbered by houze maples, although they contribute to the structural diversity of the southern and more humid portions of the prairie.

The northern Houze Prairie undergoes a gradual transition into arid steppe and semi-desert badlands. Rainfall becomes less reliable, vegetation becomes increasingly discontinuous, and exposed ground, sand, and dust become prominent features of the landscape. Houze grass persists in these environments because of its comparatively low water requirements, although its growth is less dense than in the central prairie. Extensive stands are interrupted by drought-tolerant shrubs, succulents, and patches of exposed substrate. The scarcity of woody vegetation and the relatively open character of the terrain give this region a markedly different appearance from the more productive central grasslands.

The northern prairie also experiences more pronounced temperature fluctuations. Although the region remains generally warm, its dry atmosphere and limited vegetation cover permit substantial heat loss after sunset. Seasonal cold spells are consequently more significant here than in the tropical lowlands farther south. These conditions produce an environment characterized by considerable daily and seasonal thermal variation, in contrast to the more consistently warm and humid conditions found toward the equatorial and southern portions of Ni'Khar.

Southward from the central grasslands, the Houze Prairie gradually transitions into savanna woodland and seasonal marshland. Rainfall increases, soils are generally more productive, and the duration and extent of seasonal inundation become greater. Houze grass remains widespread, but it occurs alongside a more diverse assemblage of grasses, sedges, shrubs, and broadleaf vegetation. Woody plants become increasingly common, particularly near rivers and seasonally flooded depressions. The resulting landscape is structurally more complex than the relatively open northern and central grasslands, with scattered trees, wooded drainage corridors, and extensive seasonal wetlands.

The southern transition is also where the limitations of houze grass become increasingly apparent. Its dependence on sunlight and relatively dry conditions makes it vulnerable to competition in environments with sustained moisture availability. Other grasses and broadleaf plants can outcompete it in productive soils, while persistent flooding and waterlogged ground restrict its establishment. The southern margins of the prairie consequently contain a greater diversity of vegetation than the more homogeneous grasslands farther north.

Mesa systems and broken terrain occur within the prairie, although they are not especially common. These formations interrupt the otherwise extensive lowland landscapes and create localized differences in drainage, exposure, and vegetation. Their slopes and elevated surfaces may support vegetation distinct from that of the surrounding grasslands, while their sheltered depressions can retain water after the surrounding plains have dried. They therefore contribute to the habitat's internal heterogeneity without substantially altering its overall grassland character.

The relationship between the Houze Prairie and its neighboring ecosystems is particularly evident along the margins of the Seridic wetlands. These wetlands constitute one of the most important barriers to the expansion of houze grass within Ni'Khar. Their persistently humid conditions, extensive watercourses, and dense vegetation prevent the grass from establishing the extensive stands characteristic of the surrounding plains. The Titan Gardens form another significant boundary. Unlike the prairie, these forests possess a more competitive woody vegetation community capable of maintaining dense canopy cover and limiting the sunlight available to houze grass. Their resistance to invasion demonstrates that the grass's ecological dominance is dependent upon the maintenance of open, seasonally dry environments.

Although extensive plains constitute the defining geographical feature of the habitat, the Houze Prairie is not entirely level. Mesa systems, isolated uplands, and broken terrain occur throughout its distribution, although they are not especially common. These features create localized differences in drainage, exposure, and vegetation. Elevated ground may provide refuge from seasonal flooding, while depressions and lowland basins collect water during the wet season. The resulting variation contributes to the formation of a landscape in which open grassland, temporary wetlands, and localized woodland occur in close proximity.

03 · Impact of Rain and Wind on the Prairie

Atmospheric Circulation and Precipitation

The climate of the Houze Prairie is governed primarily by tropical atmospheric circulation and the seasonal movement of monsoonal rainfall systems. Its position across the tropical latitudes of Ni'Khar places much of the habitat within a region of consistently high temperatures, where seasonal variations in precipitation are considerably more important to ecological productivity than changes in temperature. The alternation between wet and dry seasons determines the annual growth cycle of vegetation, the availability of surface water, the movement of herbivores, and the frequency of landscape-scale fires.

The surrounding oceanic environment provides an abundant source of atmospheric moisture. Warm tropical waters contribute substantial evaporation to the lower atmosphere, while prevailing winds transport this moisture toward the continental interior. The distribution of rainfall is nevertheless strongly modified by the geography of Ni'Khar. Its mountain systems intercept moisture-bearing air masses, producing substantial orographic precipitation along exposed slopes while creating comparatively sheltered environments farther inland. This contributes to the variation between humid forested regions, seasonally dry interior grasslands, and the more arid northern margins of the prairie.

The seasonal migration of the principal tropical convergence and rainfall belts is particularly important. As the zone of intense tropical convection shifts through the year, the distribution of rainfall across Ni'Khar changes accordingly. Moisture-bearing winds arriving from the south and east contribute to the establishment of the principal wet season, bringing sustained rainfall to the central and southern portions of the prairie. The movement of these systems produces a pronounced seasonal cycle, with precipitation concentrated into approximately six months of the year. The remaining six months are generally characterized by substantially reduced rainfall, although the duration and intensity of the dry season vary geographically.

The onset of the wet season is associated with the arrival of monsoonal systems from the south and east. Moist air moving inland produces widespread rainfall, particularly where it encounters elevated terrain or converging atmospheric flows. The southern and central portions of the prairie receive the most substantial precipitation during this period, while the northern regions experience more irregular and generally lower rainfall. The wet season is not necessarily uniform throughout the habitat. Its arrival, duration, and intensity vary with latitude and local topography, producing differences in the timing of vegetation growth and the availability of surface water.

The central prairie receives sufficient seasonal rainfall to support dense stands of houze grass, extensive seasonal wetlands, and substantial river discharge. Although annual precipitation is not distributed evenly throughout the year, the wet season provides enough moisture to produce a considerable increase in primary productivity. Grass growth accelerates, seasonal drainage systems become active, and temporary aquatic habitats appear across the lowlands. In the southern regions, where rainfall is greater and water remains available for longer periods, the wet season can produce extensive inundation of floodplains and marshland.

The northern prairie experiences a more severe moisture deficit. Rainfall is lower and less dependable, and the dry season can produce prolonged periods during which surface water becomes scarce. Strong winds contribute to the movement of dust and desiccated plant material across the landscape. These conditions limit the density of vegetation and favor organisms capable of enduring prolonged water shortages. The northern grassland consequently supports more open vegetation and a greater proportion of drought-tolerant shrubs and other hardy plants than the central prairie.

The transition into the dry season is generally gradual. As monsoonal rainfall retreats, smaller watercourses cease flowing, temporary ponds contract, and the larger rivers become increasingly confined to their principal channels. Soil moisture declines, and the growth of houze grass slows. Mature stems become progressively more lignified and dry, while accumulated plant material provides an increasingly abundant source of combustible biomass. The seasonal reduction in moisture is therefore directly connected to the development of the prairie's characteristic fire regime.

At the height of the dry season, fires become among the most important environmental processes operating across the grasslands. Lightning strikes, human activity, and other ignition sources can initiate fires that spread rapidly through the dry vegetation. Houze grass is particularly effective at carrying fire because its stems contain flammable oils and its dry foliage provides abundant fuel. The resulting fires can travel across extensive areas, consuming accumulated vegetation and suppressing competing plants. Although fire is destructive to individual organisms and can temporarily reduce available forage, it also maintains the ecological conditions that allow houze grass to remain dominant.

The relationship between rainfall and fire is not uniform across the prairie. In the north, low biomass and sparse vegetation can limit the continuity of combustible material, although fires may still occur where grass stands are sufficiently extensive. In the central grasslands, dense stands of houze grass provide abundant fuel, and fires can spread rapidly during the driest months. Toward the south, greater moisture availability and the presence of marshes, rivers, and more extensive woodland create natural interruptions in the distribution of combustible vegetation. These differences contribute to a spatially varied fire regime rather than a single synchronized burning event across the entire habitat.

Following the return of rainfall, the prairie enters a period of rapid regeneration. Houze grass can recover within weeks under favorable conditions, exploiting the open ground created by fire and the reduced competition from other plants. The resulting flush of growth is particularly important to grazing herbivores, which often concentrate in recently burned areas where young vegetation is more accessible. The cycle of seasonal rainfall, plant growth, drying, fire, and regeneration is consequently one of the principal mechanisms governing the ecology of the Houze Prairie.

04 · The Impact of Weather on the Prairie

Climate

The Houze Prairie is characterized predominantly by tropical and subtropical climates with pronounced seasonal rainfall. Its central and southern portions are most closely associated with tropical savanna and monsoonal conditions, while its northern margins grade into semi-arid steppe and desert environments. The climate is generally hot throughout the year, with seasonal differences in moisture availability exerting a greater influence on ecological conditions than temperature variation.

The prairie occurs within a broader tropical climatic system that includes tropical rainforest, tropical monsoon, and tropical wet-and-dry environments. The humid regions are particularly important as neighboring environments, but they are not representative of the prairie proper. Persistent moisture and the absence of a pronounced dry season favor closed-canopy vegetation, making these regions comparatively resistant to the expansion of houze grass. The prairie itself is more strongly associated with climates in which rainfall is concentrated into a distinct wet season, followed by several months of reduced precipitation.

These climatic regimes are particularly relevant to its central and southern distribution. These climates support substantial seasonal plant growth while allowing a prolonged dry interval during which vegetation becomes increasingly susceptible to fire. The precise climatic classification varies geographically, but the overall environmental pattern is consistent: high temperatures, substantial seasonal rainfall, and a dry season sufficiently pronounced to produce recurrent moisture stress.

The central prairie experiences a climate broadly analogous to the tropical savannas of sub-Saharan Africa. Its wet season generally lasts around six months, during which rainfall supports rapid grass growth and extensive seasonal flooding. The dry season occupies much of the remaining year, progressively reducing soil moisture and surface-water availability. Temperatures remain high during both seasons, although cloud cover and rainfall can moderate daytime heating during the wettest periods. The dry season is characterized by clearer skies, stronger evaporative demand, and increasingly desiccated vegetation.

The northern prairie is considerably more arid. It grades into semi-desert and steppe, where rainfall is lower and more irregular. The combination of high temperatures, low humidity, strong winds, and limited vegetation produces a landscape subject to considerable evaporative stress. Daily temperature fluctuations are also more pronounced in these exposed environments, and seasonal cold snaps occur more frequently than in the wetter central and southern regions. Although houze grass can persist under these conditions, its growth is comparatively sparse, and extensive stands are less continuous.

The southern prairie is more humid and supports a greater degree of woodland development. Its seasonal rainfall is more substantial, and surface water remains available for longer periods following the wet season. These conditions permit a more diverse vegetation structure, including scattered trees, mixed grasslands, seasonal marshes, and floodplain vegetation. The southern margins are consequently less representative of the homogeneous grasslands that characterize the central region, although houze grass remains an important component of the vegetation.

The annual climatic cycle is divided into a wet season, a transitional period, a dry season, and a period of post-drought regeneration. The wet season begins with the arrival of monsoonal rainfall from the south and east. Rivers rise, floodplains become inundated, and shallow lakes and temporary ponds appear across the lowlands. Plant growth accelerates, and the abundance of fresh vegetation supports increased activity among grazing herbivores. This is also the principal period of reproduction and dispersal for many organisms associated with the prairie.

As rainfall diminishes, the landscape gradually enters the dry season. Surface water retreats into permanent channels and deeper depressions, while smaller watercourses and temporary wetlands disappear. Grass stems mature and become increasingly resistant to digestion, while the concentration of dry plant material increases the likelihood of widespread fires. At the height of the dry season, the combination of high temperatures, reduced humidity, and abundant combustible vegetation produces some of the most severe environmental conditions experienced in the habitat.

The dry season can last approximately six months, although its severity varies with latitude and local geography. This pronounced seasonal contrast has important consequences for the movement of animals. Numerous migratory species move between northern and southern portions of the prairie as rainfall and forage availability shift. Some migrations span hundreds or thousands of kilometres, with individuals potentially spending a substantial portion of the year travelling between seasonal ranges. The timing of these movements is closely connected to the progression of the monsoon and the availability of water and fresh vegetation.

The climate is therefore not merely a background condition of the Houze Prairie but one of the principal forces governing its ecological organization. Seasonal rainfall determines when the grass grows, while the dry season creates the conditions necessary for recurrent fires. Together, these processes maintain the habitat's open structure and produce the pronounced annual fluctuations in productivity that influence its entire biological community.

05 · Fluvial impact

Hydrology and River Systems

Despite its reputation as a dry grassland, the Houze Prairie contains an extensive and seasonally dynamic hydrological network. Its rivers, floodplains, wetlands, temporary lakes, and ephemeral drainage systems are essential components of the habitat, particularly in the central and southern regions. The distribution of surface water is strongly seasonal, with the majority of smaller aquatic environments appearing during the wet season and disappearing or contracting during the subsequent dry period.

The hydrological character of the prairie is closely linked to the surrounding topography of Ni'Khar. The continent's mountain systems collect substantial precipitation and feed numerous drainage networks that descend toward the interior lowlands. Rivers originating in wetter upland environments may maintain comparatively stable flow throughout the year, while smaller tributaries and seasonal streams depend more directly on monsoonal rainfall. The resulting river systems connect humid highlands, interior grasslands, and southern lowlands, transporting water and sediment across the prairie.

During the wet season, rainfall produces a rapid increase in runoff from the surrounding terrain. Rivers rise, smaller tributaries become active, and low-lying areas are inundated. In relatively flat regions, the capacity of drainage channels to convey water is frequently exceeded, allowing floodwaters to spread across broad floodplains. These inundations create temporary wetlands and shallow lakes, many of which persist for several weeks or months before gradually receding. The extent of flooding varies annually according to the intensity and duration of the monsoon, as well as the amount of water accumulated in the surrounding uplands.

The central prairie contains some of the most extensive seasonal floodplains within the habitat. These lowland environments are periodically transformed by inundation, with channels expanding into shallow sheets of water that cover large areas of otherwise open grassland. As the dry season approaches, floodwaters retreat toward the principal river channels, leaving behind isolated pools, oxbow lakes, and shallow depressions. These residual aquatic environments become increasingly important as sources of water for terrestrial animals and as refuges for aquatic organisms capable of surviving seasonal desiccation.

The southern portions of the prairie possess a more persistent network of wetlands and marshland. Greater rainfall and more predictable seasonal flooding allow aquatic environments to remain active for longer periods. Seasonal marshes occur along river margins and in poorly drained depressions, while larger floodplains support extensive areas of shallow standing water during the wettest months. These environments form a gradual transition between the open grasslands and the more humid ecosystems farther south.

The northern prairie is hydrologically less extensive. Its lower rainfall and higher evaporative demand limit the development of permanent surface water, and many smaller drainage systems remain dry for much of the year. Water is concentrated in the principal rivers, isolated springs, deeper pools, and seasonal depressions. During particularly severe droughts, even some normally reliable water sources may contract considerably. These conditions create a more fragmented distribution of aquatic habitats than is found farther south.

The rivers of the Houze Prairie are also important corridors through an otherwise open and seasonally dry landscape. Their banks retain moisture after surrounding soils have dried, supporting vegetation that differs substantially from the surrounding grassland. Gallery forests and other riparian communities occur along sections of the larger waterways, providing shaded environments and more stable access to water. These riverine habitats are among the most important interruptions in the continuity of the prairie, both because they support distinct vegetation and because they provide refuges during the dry season.

The Seridic wetlands and their tributary systems are particularly significant in this respect. Their comparatively humid conditions support vegetation that resists the expansion of houze grass, and their persistent waterways maintain a more stable aquatic environment than the seasonal drainage systems of the prairie proper. The wetlands therefore form both a hydrological refuge and an ecological boundary to the grassland. Their tributaries connect the humid interior to surrounding lowlands, creating corridors along which moisture-dependent vegetation can persist.

The hydrological cycle also contributes to the maintenance of the prairie itself. Seasonal flooding can suppress woody vegetation in low-lying areas, while the subsequent recession of water exposes nutrient-bearing sediments and creates conditions suitable for rapid plant growth. In some locations, repeated inundation and drying may be as important as fire in maintaining open vegetation. The resulting landscape is a complex arrangement of dry grassland, seasonally flooded plains, permanent river channels, and temporary aquatic habitats.

Temporary water bodies are especially important to the prairie's seasonal ecology. Shallow ponds and ephemeral lakes provide breeding environments for numerous aquatic and semi-aquatic organisms, while their eventual disappearance favors species capable of surviving drought through dormancy, burrowing, or other forms of physiological resistance. Some aquatic organisms remain inactive beneath dry sediment until the return of rainfall, allowing them to persist through intervals during which their habitat is effectively absent from the surface.

The most conspicuous example of this adaptation is the Houze lungfish, which can survive prolonged periods of drought within protective mucus cocoons beneath the dried ground. Its capacity to remain dormant for decades illustrates the extreme seasonal instability of some of the prairie's aquatic environments. Similar patterns of dormancy and rapid reproduction occur among other organisms associated with temporary waters, allowing populations to persist despite the disappearance of their breeding habitats for extended periods.

The distribution of water also influences the movement of large herbivores. During the wet season, animals can disperse across extensive areas of flooded grassland, exploiting newly available forage and temporary water sources. As the dry season progresses, their movements become increasingly constrained by the retreat of surface water. Permanent rivers and remaining pools consequently become important concentrations of animal activity, attracting grazers and the predators that follow them. The location of reliable water sources can therefore exert a substantial influence on the seasonal distribution of the prairie's vertebrate communities.

The hydrology of the Houze Prairie is consequently characterized by strong seasonal contrasts rather than a complete absence of water. Its rivers and wetlands provide the principal persistent aquatic environments, while extensive seasonal floodplains and ephemeral water bodies emerge during the monsoon. The annual alternation between inundation and desiccation is one of the fundamental processes structuring the habitat, influencing vegetation, animal movements, and the distribution of fire across the landscape.

12 · Plant-eaters of the Houze Prairie

Herbivore Community

The Houze Prairie represents one of the most unusual and ecologically complex grassland systems in the Known World. Situated across central and northern Ni'Khar, it is not simply an extensive expanse of grassland populated by large grazing animals. Rather, it is a highly structured ecosystem in which mammals, birds, and dinosaurs have independently evolved a remarkable range of adaptations for exploiting one of Kaimere's most demanding terrestrial environments. Its herbivore community is distinguished by exceptional diversity, substantial variation in body size, and the coexistence of evolutionary lineages that on Earth would rarely occupy comparable ecological roles. Mammalian ungulates, marsupials, xenarthrans, rodents, and primates share the landscape with cursorial ornithischian dinosaurs, giant sauropods, flightless birds, and several highly specialized herbivorous lineages found nowhere else.

The defining ecological influence on this community is houze grass. Its expansion transformed much of Ni'Khar's existing prairie and seasonally dry wetland systems, creating a highly productive but nutritionally demanding food resource. The grass rapidly spread across the northern plains and into areas previously dominated by more diverse vegetation. Its extensive root systems stabilize soils, limit erosion, and allow grassland to persist in regions where rainfall is irregular. At the same time, its tough tissues and relatively low nutritional value impose substantial digestive demands on animals that depend upon it. The result is a habitat in which the ability to process abrasive, fibrous vegetation is often more important than body size alone.

The prairie is not uniformly covered by houze grass. Its vegetation forms a mosaic of tall grass stands, shorter grazing lawns, seasonal wetlands, dry river channels, scattered woodland, gallery forests, and isolated patches of more nutritious vegetation. Grass stands commonly reach one to two meters in height, creating substantial visual obstruction for animals moving through the landscape. Gallery forests and waterways are particularly important during the wet season, when they provide access to leaves, fruit, aquatic vegetation, and other foods that are scarce across the open plains. During the dry season, much of this vegetation disappears or becomes inaccessible, and the community becomes increasingly dependent on houze grass, roots, bark, and stored energy reserves.

The ecological importance of the prairie therefore changes substantially throughout the year. During the wet season, the availability of diverse foliage allows many normally specialized grazers to broaden their diets. Some species migrate toward rivers and woodland margins, while others disperse into the grassland to exploit fresh growth. As the dry season progresses, the retreat of surface water and the loss of more nutritious vegetation concentrate animals around the remaining water sources and grazing grounds. This seasonal contraction creates predictable movements and feeding opportunities, but also increases competition and exposes herbivores to predators that can exploit the concentration of prey.

The Houze Prairie's herbivores have consequently developed several distinct ecological strategies. Some are exceptionally fast and depend on early detection and sustained flight. Others survive through social cooperation, defensive formations, or formidable individual size. Several species are adapted to consuming the grass itself, while others avoid its most fibrous tissues and depend on seeds, roots, leaves, or the seasonal vegetation of the waterways. Burrowing species escape the heat and provide shelter for smaller animals, while large ecosystem engineers modify the vegetation and soil in ways that benefit entire communities. These strategies are not exclusive to mammals or dinosaurs. Both groups have independently produced fast-running grazers, large defensive herbivores, and animals capable of profoundly modifying their habitat.

The modern community is also the product of a complicated evolutionary history. Some of its members descend from animals introduced during the great harvests of Earth's Miocene, while others are descendants of lineages that survived the Dynastic Extinction or evolved elsewhere on Kaimere before returning to Ni'Khar. Still others arrived during the comparatively recent expansion of houze grass. The resulting assemblage is not a simple continuation of the ecosystems that existed before the grassland transformation. It is a comparatively recent ecological association in which old continental lineages and newly arrived specialists have reorganized themselves around an increasingly dominant plant community.

Although the Houze Prairie is often associated with its enormous dinosaurs and mammals, its herbivore community is notable precisely because no single group has achieved complete dominance. Mammalian ungulates are exceptionally diverse and abundant, particularly among the smaller and medium-sized grazers. Elasmarians occupy a similarly important position, with several lineages adapted to rapid movement and specialized grass feeding. Giant sauropods and large mammals dominate the upper end of the size spectrum, while burrowing dinosaurs, small mammals, and flightless birds exploit the lower vegetation and seed resources. The result is a grassland ecosystem whose ecological structure is more comparable to a collection of overlapping communities than to a conventional savanna dominated by a single group of large grazers.

Avian and Dinosaurian Community

The avian and dinosaurian herbivores of the Houze Prairie represent several distinct evolutionary radiations, many of which originated beyond the immediate boundaries of the Known World. Their modern distribution reflects a long history of faunal exchange between Ni'Khar and Kairul, particularly following the formation of intermittent land connections between the continents. Some lineages became established in the region during the late Miocene, while others arrived considerably later, accompanying the expansion of houze grass and its associated herbivore communities. These arrivals have produced a fauna that combines ancient Kaimeran lineages with comparatively recent migrants.

The largest members of this community are the keratoderms, a clade of titanosaurs that represents the only major surviving sauropod radiation in the Known World. Their arrival in Ni'Khar was among the most consequential events in the continent's recent ecological history. Following the Dynastic Extinction approximately fifteen million years ago, all sauropods in Ni'Khar disappeared. The continent consequently remained without its traditional giant sauropod herbivores for several million years, until the migration of keratoderms from Kairul approximately twelve million years ago. Descended from relatively small saltasaurian ancestors introduced during the Red Spring Dynasty, the lineage had evolved in an environment where competition from other sauropods remained comparatively limited. Its arrival in Ni'Khar, where the extinction had left the largest browsing niches vacant, permitted an extraordinary expansion in body size and ecological influence. Keratoderms rapidly established themselves as dominant megaherbivores, fundamentally transforming the vegetation and physical structure of the environments they occupied. Their ability to modify forest structure through sustained browsing and physical disturbance was sufficiently pronounced that their expansion contributed to major environmental and climatic changes across the Known World. These changes were among the factors that eventually prompted further Portal harvests. The modern Kipourotitanidae comprise five closely related species, all characterized by substantial osteoderm development, reinforced axial skeletons, and a distinctive suite of anatomical adaptations associated with their unusual feeding and locomotor strategies. The most imposing member of the clade is the modotan, a highly migratory sauropod that represents the largest terrestrial animal in the Known World. The species originally evolved in northern Ni'Khar, where the absence of competing sauropods permitted it to attain exceptional dimensions before populations subsequently returned to Kairul. The largest males can reach approximately twenty meters in height and exceed one hundred tons during periods of peak seasonal condition, although most mature bulls are estimated to weigh considerably less, commonly around forty to sixty tons. Its exceptional height and length make it the largest animal encountered in the Known World by linear dimensions, although its lightly constructed skeleton means i t does not approach the mass of the largest whales known from Earth.

Modotan are not conventional herd-forming sauropods. Adults are generally social, and females occasionally gather in small groups of related individuals, but mature animals do not maintain the large, cohesive herds typical of many smaller keratoderms. Their movements are extensive, and adults may travel considerable distances between feeding areas and breeding grounds. Their ability to do so is facilitated by their unusually lightweight construction, long limbs, and substantial skeletal pneumaticity. The neck is generally maintained in an elevated position, particularly while walking. During locomotion, it may curve backward so that much of its length remains approximately perpendicular to the ground. This posture is thought to reduce the muscular effort required to support the neck and may also lessen the cardiovascular demands associated with maintaining an extended horizontal posture. When feeding, individuals can extend their necks to reach suitable vegetation, although they appear to avoid maintaining this position for prolonged periods.

Cuniclosaurs, descended from orodromines, constitute one of the most abundant and diverse groups of small dinosaurian herbivores in the Known World. Cuniclosaurs are primarily fossorial animals. They excavate complex burrow systems and commonly live in loose colonies composed of related females and their mates. Most species are monogamous, with pairs remaining together for extended periods, while others form small harem-like groups containing several males. In monogamous species, males are generally only slightly larger than females. In the more socially complex species, males may be considerably larger, more brightly colored, and more aggressive toward one another. Their defensive behavior is strongly associated with the burrow. Individuals may respond aggressively to intruders approaching their colony, and males are particularly combative when defending nesting sites and territorial boundaries. This behavior has contributed to their reputation for ferocity, although it is misleading to characterize them as generally aggressive animals. Away from their burrows, cuniclosaurs are comparatively cautious and usually flee when threatened. Their defensive behavior is therefore primarily territorial rather than predatory or indiscriminately confrontational. The tsichi represents the most primitive body plan within the family and is among the smallest members of the clade. It measures approximately one metre in length and weighs only five to seven kilograms. Its body is comparatively gracile, with elongated hindlimbs and a narrow pelvis adapted for rapid movement between burrows. The forelimbs retain elongated fingers tipped with narrow claws, which are used for excavation and food manipulation. Unlike many other cuniclosaurs, the tsichi spends relatively little time underground outside the breeding season. Its colonies construct shallow burrows that can be abandoned and rebuilt within weeks as local food availability changes. The tsichi is particularly well suited to the warmer environments of Ni'Khar, including the Houze Prairie. Its plumage is relatively sparse, with only a modest layer of insulating down beneath the waterproof outer covering. The skull is lightly constructed, with a narrow beak adapted for clipping grasses, flowering plants, and fresh fern fronds. Small insects and eggs are consumed opportunistically but do not form a substantial proportion of its diet. Its feeding habits allow it to exploit the smaller vegetation growing within the prairie without relying on the nutritionally poor houze grass that dominates the upper vegetation layer.

Another dinosaurian herbivore of the prairie is the sailback runnendrake. Although historically classified among the elasmarian runnendrakes, further examination of Kairulan material has demonstrated that it is instead a dromaeodracid thescelosaur. Its superficial resemblance to the true runnendrakes reflects convergent adaptations to open grassland environments rather than close relationship. It represents the only currently known member of its clade in the Known World, although several related species have been identified in the forests and prairies of Kairul. The sailback is believed to have crossed into Ni'Khar from Kairul during the spread of houze grass, approximately 300,000 years ago. Its arrival was facilitated by the intermittent land connections that formed between the continents during periods of reduced sea level. Unlike many other thescelosaurs, which construct relatively permanent burrow systems, the sailback excavates simple and temporary dens. Individuals may use a burrow for only a few weeks before abandoning it and moving elsewhere. This comparatively mobile lifestyle is thought to have facilitated its dispersal across the land bridges. The species is an agile but not exceptionally fast runner. It is slower than many of the true runnendrake elasmarians and cannot reliably outrun young uktan, but its maneuverability and ability to enter relatively deep burrows make it difficult to capture. It wanders widely while foraging, exploiting the vegetation of the grassland and its more localized plant communities. Its presence demonstrates that the prairie supports more than a single evolutionary radiation of cursorial ornithischians, with unrelated lineages independently adapting to its open and seasonally variable environment.

The runnendrakes are a diverse clade of elasmarian ornithischians that originated in the grasslands of Arvel before spreading through Ni'Khar and eventually reaching Kairul. Their evolutionary history is closely associated with the expansion of open habitats and the development of increasingly specialized cursorial locomotion. Several lineages subsequently returned to the Known World following the expansion of houze grass, approximately 300,000 - 250,000 years ago. These returning species are among the most important dinosaurian herbivores of the modern prairie, where their adaptations to rapid movement and the exploitation of open vegetation provide substantial advantages. The podokorns represent one of the most specialized branches of this radiation. They are characterized by elongated lower legs, reduced digit number, and a locomotor system optimized for high-speed movement across open ground. Their feet are didactyl, with an enlarged third digit supporting most of the animal's weight. This digit bears a hoof-like terminal structure that functions as a shock absorber during running. The fourth digit is reduced and lacks a nail, acting primarily as an outrigger to maintain balance at high speed. The reduction in digit number lowers distal limb mass and ground friction, producing a foot that functions in many respects like a natural running shoe.

The frostback runnendrake is a relatively small podokorn. It is exceptionally lightweight, with fine, pale plumage extending over the torso, neck, and proximal tail. Its head is small and rounded, with an ornithischian beak and a comparatively short snout. The skull possesses large orbital openings and relatively large eyes, suggesting an important reliance on visual perception. Its neck is shorter than that of larger elasmarian herbivores such as Macrogryphosaurus, with lightweight cervical vertebrae reinforced around their articulations. During locomotion, the neck is commonly held in an S-shaped curve.

The bonnethead runnendrake is one of the most abundant and successful prey species of the Houze Prairie. It belongs to the fastest members of the podokorn radiation. Males possess a prominent crest that flares during rapid movement, creating a conspicuous display structure. Both sexes are exceptionally swift, capable of running at speeds comparable to or slightly greater than those of an ostrich, with substantial endurance over open terrain. Bonnetheads are particularly successful in the open grasslands, where their speed provides an effective defense against most predators. Their preferred response to danger is flight rather than confrontation, and their locomotor specialization allows them to exploit large areas of grassland while avoiding many of the larger terrestrial hunters. They are nevertheless an important prey animal. Brakunze generally find healthy adults too large and difficult to capture, but the great white cheetah frequently specializes in bonnetheads in the equatorial badlands, where they may represent one of the few reliably abundant herbivores.

The dro'khel is a considerably more formidable member of the podokorn radiation. It is a fast, aggressive elasmarian that has become highly successful within the Houze Prairie. Unlike the bonnethead, which relies primarily on speed and evasion, the dro'khel possesses an elaborate social defense system and a willingness to confront potential threats. Groups generally consist of five to twenty-five related females accompanied by a small number of unrelated males. Herds are organized around an older female, typically a single matriarch, who determines the direction of movement and the group's response to environmental conditions. Males possess thickened skin across the throat and chest, protecting vulnerable tissues during confrontations with rivals and predators. Their forelimbs are powerful and terminate in three elongated claws, while their hindlimbs can deliver exceptionally forceful kicks. Small fangs occur in males and are used primarily during contests with other males. These structures are not merely display adaptations; they provide an effective means of defending the herd against large predators. When threatened, males may turn toward the attacker while the females and young retreat behind the matriarch. The social structure of dro'khel is unusual in that the males do not lead the herd. Their principal role is defensive and reproductive, while the older females control movement and group organization. Males mate with multiple females, and because paternity is uncertain, they generally defend the young throughout the herd rather than discriminating between their own offspring and those of other males. Males are particularly intolerant of unfamiliar males and may cooperate to harass or kill intruders unless the matriarch permits their integration into the group. One of the most distinctive characteristics of the dro'khel is its resemblance to juvenile uktan, a predatory megaraptoran. The similarity is an example of an initially convergent body plan that has subsequently been reinforced through mimicry. Both animals are cursorial dinosaurs inhabiting tall grasslands, and their similar locomotor requirements initially produced comparable proportions and coloration. The structure and arrangement of their filaments also provide camouflage among houze grass, which commonly reaches one to two metres in height. As the two lineages continued to coexist, their resemblance became increasingly advantageous. Young uktan benefit from resembling dro'khel because the latter possess a reputation for aggressive defense. Potential predators may hesitate to approach an apparent juvenile uktan because its parents are likely to be nearby. Conversely, dro'khel benefit from resembling young uktan because adult megaraptorans are notably protective of their offspring. Predators encountering a dro'khel may consequently be reluctant to attack an animal that resembles a young member of one of the prairie's most formidable predators. This relationship is not purely Batesian mimicry, because the resemblance did not originate as a deliberate imitation of one species by the other. It began through convergent evolution and was subsequently reinforced by reciprocal selective advantages.

The avudi is another surviving member of the broader courser-drake radiation. Its ancestry can be traced to protoceratopsid populations that became adapted to the highlands of Ni'Khar. These animals originally occupied mountainous environments, where they foraged for nutritious taproots along spring-fed rivers. Over time, some populations developed elongated limbs, more flexible torsos, and increasingly specialized feet that allowed them to move rapidly across broken terrain. Their vision also shifted forward, improving depth perception and accuracy during leaps. The ancestral protoceratopsids possessed filamentous structures along the tail, probably serving as camouflage among shrubs. In the courser-drake lineage, these filaments gradually became more rigid and developed barbed structures, producing an increasingly conspicuous defensive display. The limbs underwent further specialization. The first and fifth toes were reduced or lost, while the second and third became more robust and hoof-like. The fourth toe remained comparatively flexible, providing additional support and balance. Elongated metacarpals and corresponding changes in the hind feet improved reach, stride length, and maneuverability. Although several related courser drakes spread throughout northern Ni'Khar and into Kairul, the avudi is the only known surviving descendant of the highland lineage that established itself in the prairie region. It is slightly smaller than the baoqu but is generally more predatory in its behavior. Riders report that avudi cannot match a horse in maximum speed, but possess considerably greater endurance, particularly when the horse is carrying a mounted rider. Groups have occasionally been observed pursuing and exhausting mounted horses. The avudi is an opportunistic omnivore whose diet changes substantially with the seasons. During the wet season, it consumes considerable quantities of vegetation, exploiting the renewed growth of the prairie. As surface water disappears and plant productivity declines, it becomes increasingly carnivorous, relying on its keen sense of smell and forward-facing binocular vision to locate animal prey. Its ability to switch between plant and animal food sources provides a considerable advantage in an environment where seasonal conditions can produce substantial fluctuations in resource availability. A darker subspecies inhabits the forests west of the prairie in the northern continent. Little is known about its population or detailed ecology, although it appears to be more solitary and considerably shyer than the open-country avudi.

The trekosaurs, or trekosaurids, represent the most extreme development of cursorial specialization among Kaimere's elasmerians. Unlike many large herbivorous dinosaurs on Earth, which became increasingly graviportal as they increased in size, trekosaurids retained exceptional acceleration and maneuverability. Their bodies are narrow and deep-chested, supported by elongated distal limbs and powerful caudofemoral musculature. The tail base slopes sharply downward and contains enlarged muscle attachment sites, allowing the tail to function as a major source of propulsion rather than merely as a counterbalance. During rapid movement, the tail and hindlimbs work together to produce a gait that may include a partially suspended running phase, an unusual capability for animals weighing several tons. In more derived trekosaurs, enlarged dorsal spines support substantial shoulder and forelimb musculature, allowing the arms to be held partially erect during movement. Males employ their forelimbs in combat, rearing partially upward and slashing downward and inward with their claws. Their chest and throat armor provides protection against these strikes. Males also possess additional tusk-like projections and fang-like teeth along the jaws, which are used primarily in ritualized combat but can also serve as defensive weapons. Their jaw mechanics favor repeated tearing movements rather than simple grinding.

The ridgeback runnendrake is one of the most successful modern representatives of the runnendrake lineage in the Houze Prairie. It returned to the Known World following the spread of houze grass. Its ancestors originated in Arvel, spread through Ni'Khar as the continents approached one another, and eventually reached Kairul before the land bridge flooded approximately eight million years ago. Its subsequent return to Ni'Khar coincided with the development of extensive houze grasslands. Some researchers have suggested that the species' abundance may have encouraged the later arrival of uktan, which found it to be a dependable source of large prey. Ridgebacks are among the most specialized consumers of houze grass. During the wet season, they migrate extensively and feed on a broader range of foliage. During the dry season, however, their diet becomes almost exclusively houze grass. Their digestive physiology changes seasonally, with an increase in gut pH that may improve their ability to process the plant's fibrous tissues. Their hooked beak is not primarily a weapon but a feeding adaptation, allowing them to grip and uproot grass more effectively. Unlike grazers that remove only the upper portions of vegetation, ridgebacks feed disruptively, often tearing up substantial portions of the plant. This behavior creates open ground that allows other plant species to establish themselves, particularly when the rains return. The dung of ridgebacks remains rich in nutrients because houze grass is difficult to digest completely. It consequently becomes an important food source for other animals during the dry season, when fresh vegetation is scarce. Juveniles also consume adult dung, both to obtain partially digested plant material and to acquire the microbial communities needed for their own digestive systems. Their early growth is rapid, with young animals increasing from approximately half a metre to 2.5 metres in height during their first year and a half. Nevertheless, they remain vulnerable to predators for approximately six years. Adult ridgebacks are among the largest herbivores of the prairie outside the giant sauropods. Mature bulls stand over four meters tall, measure approximately twelve to fourteen meters in length, and weigh between four and six tons. Both sexes possess strong forelimbs and massive claws, making them dangerous prey even for large predators. Their tusks and beaks are primarily used during male confrontations but can inflict serious injuries on predators that approach too closely. Herds generally contain animals of many ages, with numerous juveniles, mature cows, and a smaller number of adult bulls. Young animals sometimes form age-segregated groups, particularly in regions where food is limited, but the protection afforded by a larger herd generally outweighs the advantages of independent foraging.

The gurkan is a relict hadrosaur and the last known hadrosaur on either Earth or Kaimere. Once incorrectly classified as a thescelosaur, it is now recognized as a surviving member of a lineage that has persisted in the arid grasslands beyond the ' Known World. Its range is concentrated in the most desertified parts of the northern prairie, where houze grass remains abundant but competition from large mammals is comparatively limited. It lives in small groups led by an older female and defended by one to three bulls. The species is fully quadrupedal during ordinary movement, possessing a camel-like gait, although it can briefly run bipedally when a burst of speed is necessary. The gurkan is highly specialized for survival in extreme heat. Its sandy, countershaded body reflects solar radiation, while thin skin containing numerous small air pockets provides additional thermal regulation. A large display structure on the male's head is richly supplied with blood vessels and functions as a heat exchanger. A dewlap further increases the available surface area for heat loss. Both sexes possess broad sinuses that cool inhaled air, while the hollow scales of their skin allow wind to cool blood circulating through superficial tissues. These adaptations are essential in a region where temperatures regularly exceed 110°F and water may be restricted to spring-fed rivers and occasional storm runoff. Gurkan spend much of the day feeding because the low nutritional quality of their food requires continuous intake. They are hindgut fermenters, and their dung remains sufficiently nutritious that juveniles rely on it almost exclusively during their first months. Adults also practice coprophagy to recover additional nutrients. Their feet are adapted to the soft soils of the desert grasslands, with long, sharp hooves and substantial spurs on the forelimbs. These structures are used during confrontations with other gurkan and can be effective against predators. The species is larger than the taro, its principal smaller predator, and can often repel an attack if the initial ambush fails. Uktan are capable of killing adults, but their large resource requirements prevent them from maintaining many territories in the equatorial desert. Consequently, the gurkan remains one of the most successful large herbivores in this severe environment.

The avian herbivores of the prairie are dominated by the kundi, a giant flightless enantiornithean belonging to a ratite-like clade that became abundant in Kairul after the Dynastic Extinction. The kundi is unrelated to ostriches, despite its common Kaimeran name, the kaimeran ostrich. Its ancestry was only properly established through recent comparative studies by the Assembly. The name kundi means “ground bird,” specifically referring to this bird, and is the source of related names such as ro hakundi, rakundi, and harkundi. Kundi became particularly successful in the Houze Prairie following the spread of houze grass. They subsist almost exclusively on its seeds and consequently remain closely associated with the grassland. Their feathers are coated in oil and are relatively resistant to fire, an important adaptation in a habitat where dry-season fires are frequent. Nevertheless, they must flee when fires approach, as their many-chambered respiratory system is particularly vulnerable to smoke inhalation and respiratory infection. Their sickle-shaped claws are used primarily to open weak points around the entrances of termite mounds, allowing them to obtain a protein supplement without excavating the entire structure. The claws are also used during occasional disputes between individuals, although they are not particularly effective defensive weapons against large predators. When threatened, running is generally more effective than fighting.

Mammalian Community

The mammalian herbivore community of the Houze Prairie is exceptionally diverse. It includes several lineages of ungulates, marsupials, xenarthrans, rodents, lagomorphs, and primates, many of which have evolved adaptations that allow them to coexist with the dinosaurian herbivores. Their ecological success is closely associated with the ability to exploit different portions of the vegetation and to respond to the seasonal changes in food availability.

One of the most important groups is the kangaroos, particularly the two species of Osphranter that have become specialized for the prairie. Their ancestors were redback kangaroos that adapted to the expansion of houze grass approximately 350,000 years ago. The resulting species occupy somewhat different habitats and are distributed unevenly across Ni'Khar. The goldback kangaroo is the largest and most abundant of the three Osphranter species in the Ni'Khari prairie system. It is particularly common in open grassland, while redbacks remain more abundant in the badlands and silverbacks favor uplands and hinterland savanna. Large males can reach approximately forty miles per hour in short bursts, while younger males and females without large joeys may approach fifty miles per hour. Their more characteristic locomotion is a rapid hopping gait of approximately twenty miles per hour, which allows them to travel efficiently over long distances. Goldbacks are common enough that predators can exploit them regularly, although the largest males are sufficiently powerful that only confident predator coalitions are likely to attempt an attack. Females are more frequently targeted, particularly in the northern prairie where great grey cheetahs are abundant. The silverback kangaroo is more slender than the goldback, although mature males possess massive shoulders, powerful forelimbs, and heavily muscled hindquarters. Its pale silver-grey coat reflects sunlight efficiently, making it particularly well suited to exposed uplands and hinterland savannas. Silverbacks sacrifice outright speed for endurance and strength, undertaking seasonal journeys that may cover hundreds of miles as they follow rainfall and fresh grazing. Their capacity to travel efficiently through semi-arid country has allowed them to spread farther than almost any other terrestrial marsupial. The kangaroos also participate in the seasonal exchange between the prairie and the Seridic wetlands. During the final months of the dry season, water levels in the wetlands recede and extensive grasslands emerge from areas that were previously shallow marshes and swamps. Reeds are replaced by grasses and horsetails, creating temporary feeding grounds for numerous herbivores. Goldbacks and other kangaroos migrate south from the northern grasslands to exploit this seasonal abundance before the return of the rains. They join resident common hippos, water buffalo, river sloths, and deer, as well as ornithischian herbivores that enter the exposed wetlands to feed.

The houze chelotaur is one of two giant glyptodonts to survive the end-Anchored Period mass extinction. Adults of the houze chelotaur commonly exceed two tons, with their weight concentrated around the hips. This unusual body plan allows them to walk bipedally temporarily, though they are primarily quadrupedally, freeing the forelimbs for digging, uprooting vegetation, and excavating defensive trenches. Their great mass and protective anatomy make healthy adults largely immune to regular predation, although uktan and harkundi may occasionally overcome their defenses. The species has become particularly successful after adapting to houze grass. It is an obligate herbivore, and its ability to consume the abundant grass has allowed it to establish substantial populations. Its greatest source of mortality is not predation but dry-season fire, which can spread rapidly across the prairie. They are also called hovelbacks because the remains of their shells provide shelter for numerous smaller animals. Like the kangaroos, houze chelotaurs migrate into the Seridic wetlands when the seasonal retreat of water exposes grasslands and other forage. They later return to the prairie when the wetlands flood again.

Ground sloths are among the most diverse mammalian herbivores in Kaimere, and the gopher or marmot sloths are particularly abundant in the Known World. These mylodonts are perhaps descended from animals resembling Simomylodon. Unlike the larger lestodont sloths, they lack fanged tusks but possess more substantial dermal armor. Three genera and several species are recognized, with Marmotocnus being the most diverse. As in other Kaimeran sloths, males are larger than females, although sexual dimorphism is less pronounced than in the larger ground sloth lineages. Their social organization is unusual for mammals of their size. Sows may live in groups of up to thirty individuals, with their burrows frequently interconnected. Boars are more nomadic and travel between these colonies. Because their anatomy and ecological roles resemble those of bears in several respects, males and females are conventionally called boars and sows. Marmot sloths are mixed feeders, browsing and grazing on a variety of grasses and low vegetation. Like many xenarthrans in Kaimere, they are capable of digesting ferns, horsetails, and other gymnosperms more efficiently than most mammalian herbivores. This dietary flexibility is an important component of their success. They also possess a combination of dermal armor and sweat that dampens their ultraviolet signature. These adaptations may originally have evolved in response to terror birds, but they have proven equally useful against the dromaeosaurs and megaraptorans of Kaimere.

The houze marmot sloth is the smallest member of the genus and a specialized inhabitant of the central prairie. It rarely exceeds 300 pounds and possesses proportionally longer limbs, a narrower skull, and lighter armor than its relatives. These features reduce body mass and improve endurance across open terrain. It spends much of the hottest part of the day in extensive burrow systems beneath gentle hillsides, where temperatures remain relatively stable. Its colonies also provide shelter for smaller animals, creating small subterranean ecosystems within the wider prairie.

Equids are among the most successful mammalian herbivores of the Houze Prairie. Their success reflects a long evolutionary history of cursorial specialization and the development of digestive systems capable of processing tough, low-nutrient grasses. Several lineages occupy the region, and their ecological differences are sufficiently pronounced that they do not simply compete as interchangeable grazers.

The airos is one of the most conspicuous horses of the prairie. Its feral counterpart, the aranos, is similarly abundant. These large stenonine horses are exceptionally fast and possess considerable endurance. They are capable of short sprints approaching 60 miles per hour, although their more typical escape strategy involves sustained running at approximately 20 miles per hour. Their speed is sufficient to evade most mammalian predators, including veraset, which reach approximately comparable maximum sprint speeds. Airos are less agile than several of the smaller horses of the prairie. If a predator can force them into repeated turns or prevent them from establishing a sustained run, they become considerably more vulnerable. Nevertheless, their combination of speed, endurance, and ability to subsist on houze grass makes them particularly successful. Feral aranos populations are genetically diverse despite their relatively uniform appearance. Most possess some combination of white and black markings over a golden base, although occasional fertile kanos mules have introduced additional coat colors and small amounts of caballine ancestry. The prairie environment strongly favors golden coloration because it provides camouflage against predators with color vision, particularly the large theropods.

The houze garam, also called gramhos or hos, represents a separate feral horse population derived primarily from caballine stock. Its ancestors were introduced to the Crescent during the Osatran Empire approximately 3,000 years ago. Following the collapse of that empire, some populations became feral, with a portion spreading into the southern prairies. Their numbers were subsequently increased by additional imports during the Cha'Khati conquest of the Crescent approximately 500 years ago. Gramhos are generally more agile and dietarily flexible than aranos, although they lack the latter's superior speed and endurance on open grassland. This made them less competitive in the central prairie, where the ability to subsist on houze grass is particularly important. They nevertheless remain successful around settlements and on the uneven terrain of the southern Imperial territories. Regular integration with fertile kanos mules has introduced some aranos ancestry into the population, although many gramhos remain distinctly caballine.

The houze ass, also called the bruhos, is among the largest equids of Kaimere. Although it was initially classified within Equus, subsequent discoveries of related forms in Kairul and northern Ni'Khar revealed its distinct ancestry. It is believed to have descended from hipparion horses that independently evolved fully functional single-toed feet. The bruhos has a number of convergent characteristics with true equines, including the complete loss of the snort glands and the development of smaller social groups composed of related mares and a single stallion. Its behavior is notably aggressive, especially among males. Stallions readily employ their sharp hooves and elongated canines against potential threats, and the species is capable of inflicting serious injuries on predators and humans alike. Although Shu accounts occasionally claim that their warhorses were bred with bruhos, the considerable phylogenetic distance between the two lineages makes fertile hybrids extremely unlikely. The myth nevertheless persists, reinforced by reports of unusually aggressive horses with elongated canines.

The maihos is a particularly unusual member of the prairie horse community. Unlike the other houze hipparions, which returned to the Known World from Kairul, the maihos was harvested directly from Earth alongside the airos. It was among the last hipparion horses surviving on Earth at the time. Its most distinctive adaptation is a fleshy trunk, comparable in general appearance to that of the saiga antelope. This structure is thought to filter dust from the air, while also warming incoming air during cold nights and cooling it during the hottest portions of the day. The maihos is especially common in the dry, windy northern and western grasslands, where blowing sand makes conditions unsuitable for many other grazers. The species is often encountered in enormous herds, sometimes numbering in the hundreds or thousands. Its respiratory specialization allows it to exploit grasslands that other horses avoid, but the compacted skull associated with the development of its trunk appears to have reduced the space available for its nasal turbinates. Consequently, maihos are unusually susceptible to respiratory infections, particularly during the wet season. Severe outbreaks can cause extensive mortality and, in exceptional cases, the loss of entire herds.

The remaining four prairie horses belong to the genus Hippobates, commonly called the wandering horses. These animals retain three toes, although they walk primarily on the enlarged third digit, with the second and fourth digits reduced to dew claws. They possess the spring-foot condition found in numerous other horse lineages, making their retention of three toes somewhat unusual. Compared with the airos, Hippobates species are generally less proficient at sustained high-speed running. They compensate through exceptional acceleration, maneuverability, and the ability to make tight turns and abrupt stops. These characteristics are particularly useful in environments inhabited by theropods with rapid reflexes and superior turning ability. Their social organization is also distinctive. They live in fission-fusion herds composed of mare clans that maintain contact with larger groups of related or occasionally unrelated animals. Stallions are generally more tolerant of one another than those of other horse lineages and may cooperate in defending a herd. Hippobates are specialized for the Houze Prairie. When grazing, mare clans spread out rather than feeding in a single dense congregation. They generally consume the fresh upper portions of houze grass, meaning that animals following one another through the same feeding area would quickly exhaust the most nutritious growth. Larger herds therefore congregate primarily during migrations, while individual clans forage separately.

The speckled horse is the most abundant equid in the Known World. It possesses the greatest agility of the genus and forms exceptionally large social aggregations. Individual mare clans commonly number between 30 and 60 adults, while larger herds may contain several dozen or more clans and reach into the thousands. Most speckled horses possess the characteristic sunrise coat, although a melanistic variant known as the midnight sky horse is also common. The Shu associate these dark individuals with various omens and beliefs about virility and aggression, but the coloration appears to be a genetic mutation maintained by natural selection. The sunrise coat provides superior camouflage, while the midnight sky coloration may offer some protection against biting insects. The latter form generally constitutes between 10 and 30 percent of a given population.

The Kaimeran zebras, collectively called shalhos, are a pair of closely related species within Hippobates. They are somewhat less abundant than speckled horses but still occur in populations comparable to those of airos. Like other members of the genus, they live in fission-fusion herds, although their mare clans are generally smaller, commonly numbering 12–30 adults. Stallions form smaller defensive groups, typically consisting of three to five individuals.

The common shalhos is most abundant in the more humid southern Houze Prairie. The desert or mountain shalhos inhabits the more arid northern and western regions. The latter has bolder and fewer stripes, resembling Earth zebras more closely. Although it was once assumed that the pattern resulted from hybridization with African zebras, it appears to have evolved independently through the expansion of white spots. The striped coats of both species may help deter biting flies, which are especially abundant around the limited water sources of the arid prairie. This benefit may outweigh the disadvantages of reduced camouflage against predators with color vision and the possibility of increased heat retention.

The houze giant horse, or ukhos is the largest member of the genus. Mature stallions stand over two metres at the shoulder and may approach two tonnes. They are considerably less agile than their smaller relatives, but their immense size and strength make them formidable animals. Their diet is also more comprehensive, as they consume not only the crowns of houze grass but frequently take the roots as well. Strong incisors and powerful jaws allow them to process enormous quantities of tough, low-nutrient vegetation. Adult ukhos have few predators apart from uktan. Groups of two to five stallions provide formidable protection for their mare clans, and other prairie predators are generally unwilling to challenge them. Uktan, however, possess sufficient size and endurance to pursue even these massive horses. Despite their considerable size and relatively tolerant social behavior, ukhos have never become practical domestic animals. They are aggressive toward humans and respond poorly to confinement, becoming highly stressed when prevented from moving freely. A few stallions have been tamed by Shu nobles and used for parades and warfare, but captive breeding has not been successfully established. Their backs are capable of supporting a rider, although they cannot comfortably carry substantial armor, and their broad torsos make them unsuitable for long journeys. Most individuals used as mounts are therefore employed for short engagements rather than ordinary transportation.

The houze rhino is among the most successful large mammalian herbivores of the prairie. Its ancestors arrived in the Known World approximately 1.5 million years ago, but the modern species has become highly specialized for the grassland environment. Although generally lighter than the Kaimeran rhinoceros, its longer legs and larger lateral profile give it an imposing appearance. Bulls commonly exceed two tonnes, with the largest approaching four tonnes. Cows are only modestly lighter, and sexual dimorphism is less pronounced than in several other rhinoceros lineages. The most important characteristic of the houze rhino is its unusual social organization. Rather than being largely solitary, individuals live in loose congregations of related females, usually consisting of 10–30 cows and their calves. Female offspring generally remain with their mother's herd indefinitely, while young males are driven away as they approach maturity, usually around three years of age. Male groups commonly consist of three to five individuals, often distant relatives that have maintained their associations since leaving their natal herd. Adult males are generally tolerant of one another outside the breeding season. When a receptive female is present, they engage in displays of strength, including pushing, overturning rocks, and shoving down small trees. Horn swiping, stamping, and kicking are also common. Direct jousting is generally reserved for serious confrontations. The social structure of the houze rhino is especially important because the species must graze during daylight. Its diet consists almost entirely of houze grass, which provides relatively little nutrition. Unlike the Kaimeran rhinoceros, which can obtain much of its daily food during cooler nighttime hours, the houze rhino must spend considerably more time feeding. Dawn and dusk are consequently periods of particularly high predation risk. During daylight, the herds gather into defensive formations. Cows form the outer ring, with older calves positioned behind them and younger calves protected toward the center. Males either join the formation or move away to confront smaller predators before they can approach the herd. Their keen sense of smell, hearing, and surprisingly good eyesight allow them to detect threats and organize a response quickly. Houze rhinos also dig wallows to escape the heat and protect themselves from insects. Their skin folds, which may resemble armor, are primarily associated with heat regulation and the retention of cool mud. The horns and hooves are used to excavate these wallows, which can become important sources of water during the dry season. In the worst periods of drought, a wallow may be the only accessible water source for dozens of miles. Other animals subsequently depend on these excavations, making the houze rhino an important keystone species. The species is particularly vocal. Its repertoire includes chirps, honks, bellows, and a distinctive whale-like song. Most famous is a loud call that Assembly naturalists have compared to a train whistle. It is used primarily to coordinate herd movements over distance, but is also produced when uktan approach. These calls may be particularly irritating to the sensitive hearing of the megaraptorans, and Shu communities have developed whistles that imitate them. Their effectiveness is inconsistent, although they are sometimes reported to discourage approaching predators. The houze rhino has become highly specialized in consuming houze grass, although its mixed-feeder ancestry remains evident during the wet season, when leaves, fruits, and more diverse grasses become available along waterways. Its success has been considerable. It is more numerous than buffalo in many regions, although camels and elasmarians are generally more abundant. It remains an important prey species for uktan and one of the more successful rhinoceros lineages in the Known World.

The ghlanos is a giant chalicothere and one of the largest terrestrial mammals of Kaimere. Its ancestors returned to the Known World approximately 300,000 years ago, when the acratheres reappeared in the form of Acratherium. Mature stallions may reach 17 tons, although average males are generally between nine and twelve tons. Mares usually weigh between four and seven tons, with some approaching the lower end of the male range. Herds consist of related mares, their young, and one to three related stallions. Elder mares lead the herd, while the stallions provide its primary defense. The only regular predators capable of threatening adult ghlanos are uktan. Pairs of these megaraptorans can reliably bring down a large individual, although dominant stallions are sometimes able to defeat them in direct confrontations. Despite their enormous size, ghlanos retain a relatively short and sparse coat. The hair is often brightly colored, particularly in prime-age stallions, and may reflect sexual selection. Older individuals frequently lose much of their hair, with some of the largest males becoming almost entirely bald. Their coloration is also thought to be influenced by predation, as even mature stallions are not entirely safe from uktan. Ghlanos are primarily browsers, using their powerful forelimbs and retractable claws to pull down branches, dig, and uproot vegetation. At their considerable size, however, they must supplement their diet with houze grass. They are most active during the cooler portions of the day and at night, although their enormous food requirements often force them to forage during early morning and late afternoon. Reproduction is exceptionally prolonged. Mares remain pregnant for approximately two and a half years and generally give birth during the monsoon season, when food is most abundant. They may mate again after approximately six months, although some wait a year or two before reproducing again. Foals remain closely associated with their mothers for the first three years, by which time they may weigh around two tonnes. Mares generally reach maturity at approximately ten years, whereas stallions continue growing until around fifteen to twenty years of age. Young males are usually driven from the herd by the resident stallion at approximately eight years old, although their fathers may tolerate them for several additional years. These displaced males often form bachelor groups, where they practice combat and learn to defend themselves against uktan. Mortality is particularly high during this stage of life. Mature stallions frequently compete for control of herds, and a defeated male rarely survives a serious encounter. A successful stallion may retain his position for a decade or longer, but an ousted individual is often left injured and vulnerable to predators. Consequently, males rarely live beyond approximately thirty years. Females have considerably longer lifespans, with some living beyond a century. Older cows may remain as herd matriarchs, using their accumulated knowledge of migration routes, water sources, and drought refuges to guide their relatives.

The houze prairie also supports a diverse assemblage of antelopes. Although African antelopes are not the dominant herbivores in every size class, several species have successfully established themselves alongside pronghorns, horses, camels, and native hegetotheres. Their abundance is limited less by a lack of suitable adaptations than by the extraordinary number of other herbivores occupying similar ecological roles.

The eshim is a medium-small gazelle inhabiting the tallest and densest portions of the Houze Prairie. Its most conspicuous specialization is its ability to stot, or perform abrupt vertical leaps, as a means of evading predators. The hindlimbs are elongated and heavily muscled, with relatively narrow distal joints that allow considerable upward acceleration without excessive lateral movement. Its slightly elongated neck allows it to maintain visual contact with neighboring animals above the grass canopy. The muzzle is narrow and delicate, suited to selective cropping of fresh blades and tender shoots, although its cheek teeth remain sufficiently robust to process more abrasive grasses. Its pale flaxen coat blends well with the surrounding vegetation, while darker dorsal shading and lighter underparts help disrupt the animal's outline when it remains motionless.

The houze dorcas is smaller and more lightly built. It occupies semideserts, sparse prairie margins, and other dry intergrade habitats where vegetation is patchy and surface water is unreliable. Unlike the eshim, which relies heavily on explosive leaps, the houze dorcas is adapted for sustained travel and economical movement across open ground. Its body is relatively narrow, with a light chest and slender limbs. The muzzle is refined and tapering, allowing it to select forbs, short grasses, and scattered browse. Its respiratory system is particularly efficient, with extensive internal nasal surfaces that assist in recovering water during respiration. This adaptation, combined with a comparatively conservative digestive system, permits the species to survive on relatively sparse vegetation without requiring frequent access to water.

The houze oryx is the largest and amongst the most heavily built antelope of the prairie. It occupies open grassland, dry steppe, and semiarid prairie where vegetation is scattered and water may be absent for extended periods. Its barrel-shaped torso, deep ribcage, and thick neck reflect a physiology adapted to prolonged survival under arid conditions. The long, backward-curving horns are among its most conspicuous characteristics, serving in intraspecific display and defense against predators. Unlike the gazelles, the oryx is not primarily adapted for sudden evasive acceleration. Instead, it relies on endurance, efficient movement, and the ability to survive for extended periods without drinking.

The Kaimeran blue wildebeest is a heavy, broad-shouldered grazer that occupies open grassland, particularly areas where houze grass has been shortened by fire or grazing. Although it is less abundant than the pronghorns and horses, it remains an important component of the prairie herbivore community. Its massive forequarters, thick neck, and deep chest produce the characteristic sloping profile of the species. The broad muzzle is adapted for cropping large quantities of low-growing grass rather than selectively feeding on fresh shoots. Its horns form a robust defensive structure, particularly useful when individuals are crowded together in large herds. The wildebeest is a long-distance herd grazer, and its movements are strongly influenced by the availability of grazing lawns.

The Kaimeran impala is a relatively small and exceptionally lightweight antelope. Its hindquarters are substantially more muscular than its forequarters, allowing it to perform the enormous bounding leaps characteristic of the genus. The elongated ankle and metatarsal regions contribute to its jumping ability, while the mobile neck and large lateral eyes provide an extensive field of vision. Males possess lyre-shaped horns, although those of the Kaimeran subspecies are somewhat thicker at their bases and more heavily ridged than those of many Earth impalas.

Belgrave's hartebeest is a larger, high-shouldered antelope adapted to hard-packed plains and dry grasslands. Its forequarters are exceptionally tall and muscular, with strongly developed scapulae and upper forelimbs. The spine slopes downward from the shoulders toward the pelvis, producing a distinctive profile and a highly specialized running gait. Long, narrow lower limbs and compact, pointed hooves make it particularly effective on firm ground. The skull is elongated, with a narrow but deep muzzle and large nasal passages. Its horns rise outward before curving upward and inward into a broad, almost lyre-shaped structure. The coat is a striking blue-black or charcoal-grey, with a lighter underside and darker coloration across the face, neck, and upper limbs.

The giant buffalo, descended from Syncerus antiquus introduced during the African harvest, is one of the most important ecosystem engineers of the Houze Prairie. It is a massive bovid that feeds on the low-nutrient grasses that dominate the landscape. Its enormous size allows it to consume sufficient quantities of poor-quality vegetation to maintain a stable diet, a capacity that distinguishes it from most other bovines, which generally require higher-quality grass. Giant buffalo live in vast herds, moving across the prairie while grazing and fertilizing the soil. Their feeding removes large quantities of mature grass, exposing the ground and allowing other plants to establish. Their dung returns nutrients to the soil, improving productivity and increasing floral diversity. In this way, the species does not merely exploit the prairie but actively modifies its vegetation structure. Their reproductive biology is unusual for a bovid. Gestation lasts almost two years, and calves remain closely associated with their mothers for approximately two years. Even after the birth of a subsequent calf, older offspring frequently remain with their mothers for another two years. Females generally produce their first calf at around eight years of age, while males do not reach full maturity until approximately ten to twelve years. This prolonged reproductive cycle is supported by the species' complex social structure. Herds form defensive ranks around their young, with mature bulls gathering and charging when threatened. Older males can approach two tons, making a coordinated defensive charge extremely dangerous for any predator. Despite this, giant buffalo remain important prey for subadult uktan, which frequently establish their first successful family groups through hunting these large herbivores. Their distribution outside the Known World is more restricted. Although houze grass occurs throughout Ni'Khar, it is less abundant toward the equator, where the extreme heat also limits the buffalo's ability to maintain large populations. They are consequently rare in the equatorial regions. Kairulan populations may exist, although their distribution there remains uncertain. In the modern hothouse climate, any such populations would probably be relatively isolated.

The common warthog is a stocky, low-slung omnivorous mammal of prairie edges, disturbed grassland, riparian thickets, and mixed grass-country. Although not a specialist grazer, it is a regular member of the prairie herbivore assemblage and exploits a wide range of food resources. Its diet includes roots, tubers, fallen fruit, insects, and short grasses. The species possesses a large head, powerful rooting snout, and muscular neck adapted for disturbing the soil. Its prominent upward-curving tusks serve as weapons and digging implements, while swollen facial pads provide protection during combat. Its relatively long legs allow it to run surprisingly effectively across open ground, although its barrel-shaped body and low center of gravity reflect its more generalized feeding habits.

The camelids of the Houze Prairie are represented by two particularly important forms: the smaller cheni and the larger hav'reni. Both are adapted to the difficult grassland environment, although their relationships with Kaimerans differ considerably.

The cheni, or houze camelope, is a small and extremely abundant camelid. It is a close relative of the namat, the wild ancestor of the gatul, and is one of the two most important domestic animals kept by the Shu. Its name derives from the Dolani word for graceful, referring to its smooth and seemingly effortless gait. Cheni live in herds and possess considerable speed, endurance, and sensory awareness. They are particularly effective at evading the terror birds, theropods, ogres, and hyenas that hunt them. Their ability to digest tough, low-nutrient houze grass is central to their success. They are also highly prolific, allowing populations to recover rapidly from predation.

The hav'reni is a larger camelid that has become one of the fastest mammals on the prairie. Its name, given by the Dolani, describes something beautiful on the outside but ugly on the inside, an allusion to its foul temper and aggression. It is among the fastest animals of the grassland, exceeded only by harkundi terror birds and adolescent uktans. Unlike cheni, hav'reni have never been successfully domesticated. They refuse to breed in pens, often refuse food when confined, and readily bite or trample anyone attempting to contain them. They are hunted for their meat and exceptionally soft pelts, but their relationship with Kaimerans remains almost entirely exploitative. Before the arrival of horses, hav'reni were among the dominant herbivores of the prairie. Their populations have since declined relative to more rapidly reproducing equids, which are better equipped to process houze grass. They nevertheless remain important wild herbivores and are still common enough to be a valuable hunting resource.

The modern Houze Prairie contains two species of pronghorn, both of which arrived relatively recently from the Eastern Continent. Their success demonstrates the importance of locomotor specialization in an environment dominated by predators with exceptional endurance.

The common pronghorn is a relatively small species. Although it occurs in the western and northern badlands and extends into the hinterland savanna, the Houze Prairie is where it is most abundant. In particularly favorable years, it may account for as much as 60 percent of the herbivores in its size class. Common pronghorns live in small herds and are exceptionally fast. They can exceed 60 miles per hour in short bursts and maintain a cruising speed of 30–40 miles per hour over considerable distances. This combination of speed and endurance is particularly useful against the pursuit-specialist predators of the prairie. Only the veraset hyena and brakunze cheetah regularly catch healthy adults through direct pursuit, although harkundi and blue cockatrice may eventually overcome them through superior endurance. Their skeleton is unusually lightweight, with elongated lower limbs and muscle concentrated high on the legs to reduce rotational inertia. An enlarged thoracic cavity accommodates a powerful heart and lungs, while a flexible spine permits an exceptionally long stride. Males possess distinctive horns with elongated backward-curving sheaths and forward-projecting prongs. These structures are used primarily in ritualized combat during the breeding season. Common pronghorns often associate with larger grazing animals, including speckled horses and giant antelopes. Cheni are also useful companions, as their long necks and excellent hearing provide an effective early-warning system. The raised white rump patch of the pronghorn serves as a conspicuous alarm signal, visible over considerable distances.

The giant pronghorn is considerably larger. It is slower than its smaller relative, reaching approximately 50 miles per hour, but possesses extraordinary aerobic endurance. Its body is deeper through the chest, with enlarged shoulder and pelvic musculature that supports prolonged travel rather than explosive acceleration. Both sexes possess horns, although those of males are substantially larger. The horns arise from massive bony cores and sweep upward in broad arcs, sometimes exceeding a metre in length. Their pronounced forward-pointing prongs are reinforced by thick annual keratin sheaths, which provide considerable protection during intraspecific combat. A coarse tan or golden coat, white ventral surfaces, and dark facial markings provide camouflage, while a short erect mane becomes raised when the animal is alarmed.

The prairie elk is a large cervid descended from an ancestor introduced during an Asian harvest. Its closest Earth relatives appear to have been the giant Pleistocene deer, particularly Megaloceros and Sinomegaceros. The species is highly cursorial and possesses remarkable endurance, allowing it to remain active across extensive grassland even when many other large mammals struggle with the seasonal scarcity of nutritious vegetation. The body is relatively streamlined for an animal of this size, with a narrow torso, tucked abdomen, and long, lightly built limbs. Its compact hooves are particularly suited to the firm soils of the prairie, where speed and endurance are more valuable than climbing ability. The males possess enormous palmate antlers, commonly spanning 2.5 - 3.5 meters. These structures sweep laterally before expanding into broad flattened palms bearing numerous tines. Although impressive, the antlers appear to be optimized more for visual display than for forceful combat. The prairie elk's success is closely associated with its unusual seasonal feeding strategy. During the wet season, individuals congregate near rivers and consume protein-rich aquatic vegetation. This allows bulls to accumulate the nutrients required for antler growth and females to build reserves before mating. Once the dry season arrives, they can subsist for extended periods on low-nutrient prairie grass and bark. Their efficient gait, considerable fat reserves, and ability to exploit seasonal riverine resources allow them to persist in an environment that otherwise favors more specialized grassland herbivores.

Several primates inhabit the wooded patches and open grasslands of the Houze Prairie. Although they are not specialized grazers, they form an important part of the mammalian community, exploiting fruits, seeds, insects, and other resources that are less accessible to the large herbivores.

The prairie macaque is the smallest macaque inhabiting the grassland region. It spends considerable time on the ground but remains closely associated with gallery forests and isolated woodland patches. Troops move between trees in search of fruit, seeds, insects, and small vertebrates, using the trees as refuges from terrestrial predators. Its agile build and rapid movement allow it to forage in open country while retaining the climbing abilities characteristic of macaques.

The kelani is a much more unusual primate. It is noteworthy for its habitual bipedality, although it still runs on all fours when necessary. Most of its daily activity takes place on two legs, and it has lost much of its body hair, an adaptation associated with thermoregulation during daytime foraging and hunting. Kelani societies possess considerable cultural complexity. Neighboring groups maintain distinctive traditions in tool manufacture, food processing, and social behavior, transmitting these practices through learning. Stone hammers, digging sticks, sharpened branches, and woven grass carrying mats have all been documented. Although their ecological role is not that of a conventional herbivore, their ability to exploit plant foods and modify their environment makes them a significant component of the prairie ecosystem.

The Kaimeran baboon is the most common primate of the Houze Prairie. It is a subspecies of the modern olive baboon and is comparable in size and general lifestyle to its Earth relative. Although particularly abundant in the grasslands, it is found throughout much of the Northern Continent, excluding wetlands. Its omnivorous diet allows it to exploit fruits, seeds, roots, insects, and small animals, while its social organization provides protection against predators.

The prairie hare is the largest living lagomorph in the Known World, commonly weighing between 20 and 30 pounds, with exceptional males exceeding 35 pounds. Its grey-brown coat, lighter flanks, and nearly white underside provide disruptive camouflage among dead grass, soil, and shadow. Its unusually elongated vertebral column and powerful hindlimbs permit sustained running across open terrain, with short bursts exceeding 75 kilometres per hour. The ears are exceptionally long, often approaching one-third of the animal's body length. Their extensive blood supply allows them to function as radiators, dissipating heat during prolonged activity beneath the prairie sun. The darkened tips may also absorb solar radiation during cooler mornings, although their primary function appears to be thermoregulation. Although vegetation remains the majority of its annual food intake, the prairie hare is unusual among lagomorphs in its willingness to consume animal matter. Its enlarged jaw musculature and more heavily reinforced skull allow it to process tougher grasses, while its incisors possess thicker enamel to withstand abrasion. It will opportunistically hunt rodents, insects, nesting birds, and small reptiles, particularly during droughts when fresh vegetation becomes scarce. Its digestive system remains fundamentally that of a hindgut fermenter, although the stomach produces unusually concentrated digestive acids and a broader range of protein-digesting enzymes than those of other hares.

Among the most distinctive small-to-medium-sized herbivores of the northern prairie are the hegetothere antelope jackalopes. These notoungulates have developed several adaptations for life in exposed grassland, including exceptional running ability, high reproductive output, and specialized nasal anatomy.

The chemaimai, or trunked jackalope, is particularly abundant in the northern prairies. Its trunk resembles that of the maihos, the trunked hipparion horse, although the two are not closely related. The chemaimai possesses a posterior extension of the palate and a nasal opening positioned farther back in the skull. This arrangement increases the space available for the nasal turbinates, allowing the animal to filter bacterial particulates while the trunk conditions incoming air and prevents the inhalation of sand and dust. The chemaimai does not appear to suffer unusually high rates of respiratory infection. Its nasal anatomy provides many of the benefits of a trunk without the same apparent reduction in turbinate efficiency. Combined with relatively large litters and exceptional endurance, these characteristics have contributed to its abundance. In some northern regions, chemaimai are the most numerous herbivores.

The common jackalope dominates the mixed prairies and scattered woodland margins of central Ni'Khar. The species forms immense migratory herds, sometimes numbering in the thousands. Its principal defenses are explosive acceleration, erratic bounding, and exceptional maneuverability. Individuals frequently change direction in mid-leap, making them difficult for pursuing predators to intercept.

The greater jackalope inhabits the drier northern grasslands. Its most distinctive feature is the enormous scimitar-shaped horns of mature males, which may exceed five feet in length. These structures are used in display and defense against medium-sized predators. They also provide visual signals that help maintain herd cohesion across open country.

The burrowing beaver lineage Paleocastor once had a considerable presence across Ni'Khar and Kairul. Its corkscrew burrows provided insulation and protection in the often intensely hot grasslands. Although many species were eventually replaced by lagomorphs and ground squirrels, one small descendant remains in the Houze Prairie: the biting sloth. The biting sloth is a small burrowing beaver whose name derives from its habit of excavating soil with its teeth. Its superficial resemblance to the prairie sloths led to an early and mistaken identification as a small mylodont. In reality, it is a rodent, and its ancestry is among the few surviving remnants of the former diversity of Paleocastor. Its winding underground tunnels are excellent insulators, allowing it to remain active in the high temperatures of the prairie.

The greater houze jerboa is a small saltatory rodent inhabiting dry prairie, sandy flats, and sparsely vegetated scrub. Its hindlimbs are dramatically elongated, permitting rapid leaps across open ground, while its long tufted tail acts as a dynamic stabilizer during abrupt changes of direction. Enlarged auditory bullae improve its ability to detect approaching predators across exposed terrain.

The lesser prairie jerboa is smaller. It favors firmer soils and open shrubland, where its comparatively shorter hindlimbs and compact skull are better suited to excavation. It constructs extensive burrow systems and spends much of the hottest portion of the day underground.

13 · Meat-Eaters of the Prairie

Carnivore Community

The Houze Prairie of central Ni'Khar supports one of the most diverse and vertically stratified terrestrial carnivore communities on Kaimere. Its predator assemblage is the product of an unusually complex evolutionary history, involving indigenous Kaimeran lineages, successive introductions from Kairul, and several waves of animals harvested from Earth. These organisms have entered an environment that has been profoundly transformed by the expansion of houze grass, a highly productive but silica-rich and nutritionally poor grass that now dominates much of the region. Although the grasslands support immense populations of grazing mammals, the difficulty of digesting houze grass imposes considerable ecological constraints on herbivores, concentrating much of the available animal biomass into a relatively small number of successful grazing lineages. The resulting abundance of large, mobile prey has allowed the prairie to sustain an exceptionally diverse community of predators.

The structure of this community is not adequately represented by a conventional trophic pyramid. Predators are separated not only by the size of their prey but also by hunting strategy, activity period, social organization, habitat preference, and their capacity to defend kills against competitors. A prairie hyena, for example, may be an extremely successful predator of ungulates while remaining vulnerable to a much larger theropod. A cheetah may occupy the uppermost level of mammalian predation but still lose its kill to a terror bird or megaraptoran. Even the largest predators are dependent on smaller carnivores to process carcasses, regulate populations of smaller animals, and exploit ecological opportunities that their own size and hunting requirements prevent them from using.

Kaimeran naturalists commonly describe these relationships through the terminology of officers, vassals, and apex predators. The terminology originated from an ecological analogy that compared a functioning ecosystem to a feudal hierarchy. In this interpretation, officers occupy the lower ranks of the ecological nobility, functioning as the dominant predators within their respective size classes and often exerting substantial pressure on herbivore populations. Vassal predators are considerably larger, generally falling between approximately 450 kilograms and two tonnes, and occupy an intermediate position between familiar large mammalian predators and the enormous theropods that dominate the ecosystem. Apex predators, or monarchs, occupy the highest position, possessing the size and ecological influence to prey upon most other terrestrial carnivores as well as the largest herbivores.

These categories are descriptive rather than rigid biological classifications. A predator's position can vary geographically, and the same species may occupy different ecological roles depending on which competitors are present. The Houze Prairie demonstrates this particularly well. The enormous megaraptoran Thanatobates uktan dominates the open grasslands, but its extended parental care leaves much of the smaller predator niche available to other species. Consequently, the prairie supports a diverse assortment of officers and vassals that would otherwise be displaced by juvenile megaraptorans. The result is a community in which mammalian carnivores, cockatrices, terror birds, and large omnivores coexist through a complicated combination of competition, temporal separation, cooperative hunting, and specialization.

The seasonal character of the prairie further shapes this arrangement. During the wet season, abundant grazing attracts large concentrations of herbivores, allowing predator populations to exploit plentiful prey. During the dry season, water becomes scarce, vegetation deteriorates, and migrating herbivores concentrate around permanent water sources and wetlands. Predators that remain on the prairie must defend territories, pursue increasingly dispersed prey, or turn to scavenging and alternative food sources. Some species become more social during these periods, while others exploit the vulnerability of animals gathering at the remaining water sources. The carnivore community is therefore not simply a collection of predators occupying different trophic levels, but a dynamic system whose relationships change considerably throughout the year.

Lesser Predators of the Prairie

The Kaimeran wildcat is the most widespread small cat in the Known World. Descended from African wildcats, it rapidly expanded through Ni'Khar and other regions because of its adaptability. Unlike specialized cats restricted to a narrow habitat or prey type, the wildcat can occupy grassland margins, woodland, scrub, and human settlements. It has outcompeted or placed considerable pressure on several smaller native cats, including leopard cats and small nimravid lineages. Although not a major predator of large herbivores, it is an important hunter of rodents, small birds, reptiles, and other animals inhabiting the prairie margins.

The sand badger is a native taxidiine mustelid and one of the last surviving representatives of a formerly diverse lineage. It is an accomplished burrower, specializing in locating and excavating subterranean prey. Although once more widespread, the clade has been reduced by climatic change and faunal turnover. The arrival of the honey badger appears to have contributed substantially to its decline, and the sand badger may now be the last surviving member of its lineage in the Known World.

The honey badger is among the most successful small mustelids of the prairie. It was probably introduced from northern Africa, although an earlier Asian introduction cannot be ruled out. It is an accomplished digger and obtains much of its food by excavating burrows. Rodents and reptiles, particularly lizards and snakes, are common prey, although it is highly opportunistic and will consume almost anything it can overpower or scavenge. Its success has placed considerable pressure on the remaining sand badger populations.

The Houze hedgehog is a small insectivorous mammal occupying open grasslands, shrublands, abandoned burrows, and agricultural margins. It is not a significant predator of vertebrates. Its elongated snout and enlarged olfactory chamber help it locate insects beneath the dense prairie sod. Longer limbs than those of its African ancestors improve terrestrial movement, while its flexible dorsal spines allow it to wedge itself into thick vegetation while remaining protected from predators. It is an important consumer of insects and other small invertebrates, and is itself prey for numerous small carnivores.

Officers of the Prairie

Officer predators constitute the most numerous and ecologically diverse tier of the large-predator community. They include the familiar mammalian carnivores of Kaimere, alongside smaller theropods and other predators that occupy comparable ecological positions. Most are capable of killing substantial herbivores, and several regularly hunt animals larger than themselves. However, they remain vulnerable to the larger vassal predators and apex megaraptorans that dominate the prairie. Their success consequently depends upon exploiting prey efficiently, avoiding unnecessary confrontations, and making use of opportunities that larger predators cannot always pursue.

The most influential mammalian officers are the prairie hyena, the veraset, the Kaimeran cheetahs, and the leopard. These species occupy overlapping but distinguishable ecological niches. Their respective hunting strategies illustrate the importance of specialization in an environment where the largest predators are capable of appropriating kills from almost any smaller carnivore.

The prairie hyena is among the most abundant and successful large mammalian predators of the Houze Prairie. Descended from the hyenas of Kairul, it became established in Ni'Khar alongside the spread of houze grass and the associated transformation of the prairie herbivore community. Its success is attributable to its combination of physical power, considerable endurance, intelligence, and unusually sophisticated social organization. Prairie hyenas possess a powerful build, strong jaws, and the ability to sustain pursuit over considerable distances. Their coloration is variable, with gold, silver, light brown, white, and tan individuals occurring throughout the population. Muted blond coloration is particularly common in the open grasslands, where it provides effective camouflage among the pale stems of mature houze grass. Their reputation as scavengers is misleading. Although they readily consume carrion and are exceptionally proficient at extracting nutrients from bones, their scavenging behavior is largely a consequence of their activity patterns. Most hunting occurs at night, and individuals encountered by human observers during daylight are frequently resting or feeding on kills made during the preceding evening. The species' principal advantage is its social intelligence. Prairie hyenas live in fission-fusion clans that may contain more than one hundred adults, although the entire clan rarely hunts together. Instead, hunting is generally undertaken by smaller groups of three to five individuals. These groups may maintain stable membership and develop preferences for particular prey species. When preferred prey becomes abundant, experienced hunters can concentrate their efforts on it, while other members of the clan exploit alternative resources. This flexibility allows a large clan to make use of a wide range of prey without requiring every individual to participate in every hunt. A substantial proportion of adults hunt alone. This is particularly common among lower-ranking individuals, which may receive little access to a carcass when hunting with more dominant clan members. Solitary hunting allows these individuals to retain the entirety of their kill, although remaining within the clan provides important advantages in territorial defense and protection against larger predators. Cooperative hunting becomes more common during periods of ecological stress, when large or dangerous prey must be brought down to secure sufficient food. The social hierarchy of prairie hyenas is frequently described as matriarchal, although this interpretation is an oversimplification. Social rank is primarily determined by an individual's relationship to the dominant female, or matron. Since males generally leave their natal clans to reproduce elsewhere, they are commonly unrelated to the matron and occupy lower positions in the hierarchy. A male born to the matron, however, may remain in the clan and retain a high social position into adulthood. Such individuals can temporarily become the highest-ranking members following their mother's death, although the clan generally reorganizes under another matron or fragments into smaller groups. Prairie hyena dens are complex structures, often incorporating multiple entrances, chambers, and concealed approaches. Their defensive behavior is particularly sophisticated. When threatened by another clan or a larger predator, members coordinate feints, diversions, and simultaneous attacks. These strategies are sufficiently elaborate that Shu military commanders have historically studied hyena defensive behavior, and several Imperial military terms derive from the tactics used by these animals. Prairie hyenas are also notable for their relationships with other predators. Some clans maintain long-term associations with blue cockatrices, particularly with individual females and their respective matrilines. The hyena contributes greater physical strength and speed, while the cockatrice provides endurance, visual acuity, and formidable weapons. Together, they can overwhelm prey that would be difficult for either species to capture independently. The closest relationships are often between a hyena matriarch and a cockatrice hen, and some associations persist across generations. Such cooperation is not universal, however, and neither species is necessarily committed to maintaining the partnership during periods of severe scarcity. Hyenas have also been recorded cooperating with harkundi. These associations are generally opportunistic and vary according to local conditions. Their ability to accommodate other predators is one of the reasons prairie hyenas have become so successful across Ni'Khar. They are not simply powerful hunters, but adaptable social animals capable of exploiting a wide range of ecological relationships.

The veraset, or running hyena is another highly successful mammalian predator of the Houze Prairie. It belongs to a cursorial hyena lineage descended from Chasmoporthetes. While the ancestral lineage was already adapted for pursuit, subsequent evolution increasingly emphasized speed, producing one of the fastest mammalian predators on Kaimere. The veraset can reach approximately 55 - 60 miles per hour in a sprint. Its build is adapted for rapid acceleration and high-speed pursuit, and it specializes in capturing the fastest herbivores of the prairie. This is particularly advantageous in an ecosystem where many prey species have evolved exceptional endurance to escape the sustained pursuits of theropods. Whereas megaraptorans and other large theropods commonly rely on maintaining a steady pace until prey becomes exhausted, the veraset attempts to end the pursuit before its own energy reserves are depleted. Its speed allows it to exploit prey that are difficult for most other predators to catch. Pronghorns are among its most important targets, and it is one of the few predators capable of regularly capturing them. It also pursues other swift ungulates, including antelope and smaller horses. The species is a comparatively successful hunter, but its high-speed pursuits are energetically expensive. After a prolonged chase, an individual may be too exhausted to defend its kill against larger competitors. This vulnerability has contributed to the veraset's reputation as an unsuccessful competitor. Common hyenas, harkundi, and young uktan frequently displace individuals from their kills. The veraset is not physically helpless, possessing a powerful bite and considerable strength, but the cost of fighting after a strenuous pursuit is often greater than the cost of abandoning the carcass and hunting again. During the dry season, or when hunting in small coalitions, verasets are more likely to defend their kills. Nevertheless, their success as hunters compensates for many of these losses. The veraset's principal ecological advantage is its specialization in speed. Most other prairie predators rely on endurance, strength, or ambush, leaving the fastest prey comparatively inaccessible. The veraset exploits this gap and has persisted where numerous other predators of the pre-houze grassland disappeared. It is consequently regarded as one of the most important surviving representatives of the older prairie carnivore community.

The Kaimeran cheetah, commonly called the pard or brakunze, is a successful mammalian pursuit predator across western Ni'Khar and the northern grasslands. Its ancestry is probably mixed, involving a smaller native cheetah lineage and African cheetahs harvested approximately 250,000 years ago. Although it closely resembles the modern cheetah in general appearance and proportions, genetic and skeletal evidence supports its treatment as a distinct species. Brakunze generally weigh between 20 and 40 kilograms, with large males occasionally reaching approximately 60 kilograms. They are among the most effective hunters of small and medium-sized ungulates on the prairie. Their success is based on speed, acceleration, and an exceptionally high rate of successful hunts. Unlike many of the larger predators, they do not attempt to defend every kill. Instead, they consume as much as possible before competitors arrive and then retreat. This strategy is particularly effective in an ecosystem where larger predators can detect a chase from considerable distances and may arrive before a smaller carnivore has finished feeding. Brakunze are especially successful in the open and arid northern prairie, where pronghorns, gazelles, antelope, and jackalopes provide abundant prey. Their diet also includes small runnendrakes and cheni camels. Males in coalitions are capable of pursuing larger prey, including desert shalhos, sailback runnendrakes, and golden kangaroos. Speckled horses have occasionally been killed, although even coalitions rarely attempt prey substantially exceeding 200 kilograms. The tall houze grass provides important advantages for hunting cats. It conceals their approach and makes prey less likely to identify the precise location of an attack. It also provides suitable denning cover, and cub survival on the northern prairie can be higher than in more exposed wooded savannas. The deep root systems of houze grass stabilize the soil, reducing the amount of dust raised during a chase and helping prevent the hunter's location from being revealed. Brakunze are generally solitary, although approximately 60 percent of males form coalitions. These groups commonly develop around scent-marking sites and may include unrelated individuals. Females are nomadic and maintain large ranges, particularly when they have no secure den. The species is not especially territorial, and scent markers function primarily as communication sites rather than permanent territorial boundaries. Coalitions may defend access to a marker or a particularly abundant food source, but individuals frequently travel considerable distances. Their reproductive strategy differs somewhat from that of other large cats. Females can produce relatively large litters and reproduce frequently, allowing populations to recover rapidly following periods of high mortality. Cub mortality remains substantial, especially in areas with little protective cover, but brakunze are notably tolerant of unrelated young. Adoption of orphaned cubs by females with litters of their own is well documented, and adults of either sex may temporarily accept weaned juveniles. There are no confirmed records of adults deliberately killing cubs of their own species. The species' success is also associated with its willingness to abandon kills. Brakunze generally lose only a minority of their kills to competitors, and their hunting success is sufficiently high that abandoning a carcass is often less costly than fighting. This behavior is especially useful in the Houze Prairie, where a cheetah attempting to defend a kill might encounter a hyena clan, a cockatrice flock, a harkundi, or a megaraptoran. Their strategy is therefore not a sign of weakness, but a specialization for exploiting prey quickly in an ecosystem with numerous larger competitors.

The great white cheetah is a much larger relative that occupies the northern Ni'Khari deserts and western badlands. It is especially successful in the open, arid landscapes beyond the Known World, where its long limbs, slender build, and efficient stride help dissipate heat and sustain long pursuits. Males may reach approximately 170 kilograms. Although capable of considerable speed, the species is particularly notable for its endurance and ability to maintain a steady pace over long distances. Great white cheetahs hunt elasmarians, kangaroos, jackalopes, desert shalhos, and other medium-to-large herbivores. They also exploit hares and smaller game when larger prey is unavailable. During migrations of large herbivores, individuals have been observed forming larger, potentially mixed-sex hunting coalitions. Their skulls are more robust than those of brakunze, with larger canines that assist in handling substantial prey, although they remain pursuit specialists rather than animals adapted for prolonged physical contests over carcasses. Their coats have lost most of the spotting characteristic of other cheetahs. Adults commonly retain only a few spots on the limbs, and males sometimes develop a mane, particularly when traveling in coalitions. These features have led to historical confusion with lions, and some accounts of enormous lions in northern Ni'Khar may actually describe great white cheetahs. Juveniles possess more conspicuous spotting and may also account for some reports of leopards in regions where the species is otherwise uncommon. Great white cheetahs are rare in the hinterland savannas but become more common farther north. They have not established themselves in Kairul, despite historical opportunities for dispersal, probably because a similar cursorial predator occupies the corresponding ecological niche there.

The Ni'Khari leopard is one of the most widespread large cats in Kaimere, although its presence on the Houze Prairie is restricted by the scarcity of suitable tree cover. Unlike lions and several other large African carnivores, the leopard was able to adapt to the more competitive predator community of Kaimere. Its success derives partly from its behavior as a subordinate predator. On Earth, leopards frequently drag their kills into trees to avoid losing them to larger competitors. In Kaimere, this behavior has proved particularly advantageous in an ecosystem dominated by large theropods and highly social mammalian carnivores. Leopards are capable of exploiting prey while avoiding direct competition with the largest predators, and their ability to use trees allows them to retain food that would otherwise be appropriated by hyenas or cockatrices. The Ni'Khari leopards size varies geographically, with large males in western forests reaching approximately 100 kilograms. Prairie individuals may be comparable in size to large Indian leopards, although many are smaller. Melanism is uncommon in the open prairie but occurs more frequently in western forest populations, where dense vegetation and the relative scarcity of tigers provide favorable conditions. Leopards are particularly successful in the wooded margins of the Houze Prairie and in the more open forests to the south and west. They compete with the lesser Ni'Khari pouch-lion and other large cats in the savannas between the prairie and the Crescent. In the open grassland itself, however, they are generally outnumbered by more specialized pursuit predators and are rarely found far from cover.

The lesser Ni'Khari pouch-lion, or drop bear, is a marsupial predator that occurs throughout much of Ni'Khar. It is the most widespread species of pouch-lion and is particularly associated with wooded margins, gallery forests, and areas containing suitable denning habitat. Males average approximately 100 kilograms, while females commonly weigh between 80 and 90 kilograms. Unlike conventional cats, Thylacoleo possesses powerful forelimbs with an opposable thumb and strongly developed claws. These arms allow it to grapple prey at a considerable distance from its body, an advantage when confronting large ground sloths with formidable defensive claws and armored necks. Its bite is also exceptionally powerful for its size, allowing it to kill prey quickly once a secure hold has been established. Pouch-lions have developed a particular association with mylodont sloths. Their ability to reach around defensive claws and attack vulnerable regions has allowed them to prey upon sloths that many other predators avoid. They can even raid sloth dens, where they may kill an animal and feed on it over several days. They generally target the organs first, a feeding behavior consistent with the selective consumption of viscera observed in their Earth ancestors. The drop bear frequently caches kills in trees, a behavior probably inherited from ancestors that lived alongside crocodilians and large terrestrial lizards. It is not as powerful as the larger pouch-lion species, but can kill prey considerably larger than itself. Its willingness to explore the vicinity of settlements makes it one of the more dangerous mammalian predators to people. Individuals may overpower adult humans, particularly when surprised or encountered at close range. Their distinctive reddish-brown backs, black lateral stripes, and soft cream-to-golden belly fur have also made them desirable targets for hunters. Pouch-lions are not apex predators. Their relatively limited speed and tendency toward nocturnal activity make them vulnerable to larger predators, particularly terror birds. They generally avoid open ground and favor cover, where their powerful forelimbs and ambush tactics are most effective. Their kills also support a distinctive scavenger community, including mustelids, hyenas, and young theropods that enter the tunnels after the pouch-lion has finished feeding.

The pardhund or panther hound is a gracile borophagine predator that occupies the open prairie. Unlike the robust descendants of Epicyon, which were largely displaced by the common hyena, these animals evolved toward cursorial pursuit and cooperative hunting. They are described by Assembly naturalists as exceptionally aggressive hunters, with a combination of speed, endurance, and powerful jaws that allows them to attack prey much larger than themselves. Panther hounds hunt in large groups, commonly numbering a dozen or more. Their jaws are not primarily adapted for breaking bones, but for maintaining a powerful grip and tearing at prey. A group can overwhelm an animal through repeated attacks, inflicting numerous wounds until the victim can no longer escape. They are among the few mammalian predators, alongside the veraset, capable of regularly capturing pronghorns. Their hunting strategy requires rapid consumption. Once prey has been brought down, individuals feed intensely, with their elastic abdomens allowing them to nearly double their body mass within minutes. They then retreat to their dens before larger predators arrive. This behavior is particularly important because panther hounds are themselves vulnerable to both hyena species, terror birds, and cockatrices. Their tenacity and coordinated mobbing make them dangerous opponents, but most larger predators avoid confronting a full pack unless the potential reward is considerable.

The most successful derived cockatrice lineage in the Known World is the blue or prairie cockatrice, which arrived from Kairul approximately 1.5 million years ago during a period of low sea levels. Also commonly called the fosnu, is particularly successful on the Houze Prairie. It arrived from Kairul alongside common hyenas and uktan and has become an important predator of the prairie herbivore community. Its coloration is variable, with blond individuals particularly common in open grasslands and brown morphs more frequent in wooded regions. Males tend to be larger and darker, with more prominent crests, although the sexes overlap substantially in size and females also possess crests. The fosnu is considerably more social than the common cockatrice. Its family groups may contain several generations of the same matriline, and leaders of these groups sometimes establish long-term associations with the matriarchs of prairie hyena clans. These relationships can produce highly coordinated hunting partnerships. The hyena contributes greater speed and physical power, while the cockatrice provides endurance and formidable weaponry. Both possess excellent senses of smell, allowing them to coordinate effectively even in dense grass. The blue crest is also used in hunting. Some prey species are colorblind and cannot perceive its blue coloration, while others can see it and may be startled by its conspicuous display. The fosnu may exploit this response to provoke prey into fleeing, after which it can maintain a pursuit until the target becomes exhausted. This strategy is particularly effective in the Houze Prairie, where the tall grass can conceal the flock's approach.

The tank jackal is a predatory armadillo descended from the genus Macroeuphractus. It has become successful in Kaimere following the expansion of grasslands and the ecological changes associated with a major cooling period. Its principal adaptation is burrowing, which allows it to exploit prey that larger predators cannot readily reach. Small sloths, rodents, snakes, and burrowing thescelosaurs form important components of its diet. The abundance of subterranean prey in the Houze Prairie provides it with a relatively secure food source, reducing direct competition with the larger pursuit predators.

The lion sloth occupies an unusual position within the carnivore community. It is a lestodont ground sloth and one of the most predatory members of its clade. Meat makes up a substantial proportion of its diet during the dry season, when the deterioration of vegetation and the low nutritional value of houze grass make conventional herbivory less profitable. Its digestive physiology has become increasingly adapted to carnivory, with a reduced gut pH that makes the digestion of houze grass difficult. During the wet season, however, plant matter again constitutes most of its diet. Its carnivory is therefore best understood as a seasonal response to nutritional stress rather than a permanent dietary specialization. The lion sloth is surprisingly cursorial, capable of sprinting at approximately 25 miles per hour. It commonly hunts at night, using ambush to capture animals before they can accelerate. Nevertheless, much of its meat is obtained through scavenging, particularly from hyena and cockatrice kills. Its strong forelimbs and claws are useful for digging, and both sexes construct simple burrows in which they shelter during the heat of the day. Females may live in groups and sometimes share a den, although their burrows are often separate tunnels branching from a common system. The species is sometimes classified as a subspecies of the red-furred wojun because the two can interbreed where their ranges overlap. Most naturalists nevertheless retain Vulgarocnus daimonleo as a distinct species. Its yellow pelt, skeletal proportions, and dietary adaptations distinguish it from other members of the group. Pronounced caniniform teeth are characteristic of the wider lestodont clade, and in some relatives these teeth extend beyond the mouth as tusks. Among Kaimeran lestodonts, these structures are used in combat, defense, access to difficult foods, and occasionally in conjunction with the foreclaws during digging.

The na'hashet is an entelodont of the highly derived subfamily Synkanninae. Although omnivorous, it has a strong preference for meat and is a significant participant in the prairie predator community. Its fused metatarsals and metacarpals form a cursorial limb structure that allows it to run at approximately 45 miles per hour in a short sprint and maintain a jog of around 15 miles per hour over distances of 10–15 miles. It is also an accomplished leaper, capable of clearing obstacles as high as three metres. The na'hashet possesses an unusually flexible vocal apparatus. Its U-shaped larynx, muscular vocal structures, and small laryngeal air sacs allow it to produce a remarkable range of sounds. Individuals frequently imitate sounds they hear while resting or bored, and may reproduce the calls of prey to draw animals closer. Those living alongside prairie hyenas can imitate their companions' vocalizations, allowing them to participate in coordinated activity and potentially summon allies. They may also mimic the distressed calls of horses or even human children to lure prey or unsuspecting people away from safety. Na'hashet frequently associate with prairie hyena clans. They are highly intelligent, socially adaptable animals, and their ability to imitate hyena calls makes them particularly effective companions. They may cooperate in hunting, scavenging, or territorial defense, although they are not entirely dependent upon the relationship. Their omnivory allows them to survive on a wider range of foods than most large prairie carnivores, an advantage in an environment where plant resources are limited and seasonal.

Vassals of the Prairie

Vassal predators occupy the intermediate tier between the officers and the enormous apex predators. They are generally larger than the largest familiar terrestrial predators of Earth, commonly weighing between approximately 450 kilograms and two tons, although the category is not defined by strict body-mass limits. They are powerful enough to threaten most officers, and many are capable of killing large herbivores that would be difficult or impossible for smaller predators to subdue. Nevertheless, they remain substantially smaller than the great megaraptorans that dominate the prairie.

The Houze Prairie has an unusually diverse vassal community. Its most important members are the harkundi terror bird and the taro megaraptoran, although large cockatrices also occupy parts of this ecological tier. These predators are not simply enlarged versions of the officers. They have distinct hunting strategies and often exploit prey or activity periods that reduce direct competition with one another. Their relationships with the uktan are particularly important, as the apex predator's prolonged parental care allows these species to persist in niches that would otherwise be occupied by juvenile megaraptorans.

The ok'hos, or scavenger king, is the largest and most imposing member of the cockatrice community described in the supplied account. It is relatively uncommon, but its intimidating appearance allows it to dominate many carcasses. Its scent glands produce a smell resembling decomposition, while its body is covered in quills and its facial integument resembles exposed entrails. Its wings are used to assert dominance at kills, and the ok'hos takes this behavior to an extreme, using its quills and powerful claws to discourage competitors from approaching. Although capable of hunting and occasionally running down horses or other ungulates, the ok'hos is primarily a formidable scavenger and kleptoparasite. However, their size and weight places them within the classification of vassal predators. Most predators, including harkundi, will retreat when one arrives at a carcass. Uktan are among the few animals that consistently challenge it. The species is highly intolerant of other ok'hos, although rare pairs or small groups have been observed. Such groups can be so intimidating that even an adult uktan may abandon a kill. Ok'hos sometimes associate with hyenas, blue cockatrices, vultures, and keynu, forming loose entourages that harass other predators and exploit carcasses. These relationships are not necessarily permanent or cooperative in the strict sense, but they provide considerable advantages to smaller scavengers that accompany the scavenger king.

The harkundi is among the most abundant and ecologically influential predators of the Houze Prairie. A large flightless terror bird, it is highly specialized for pursuit and occupies a position as one of the dominant vassal predators of the open grasslands. Its name, derived from the language of the Dolani nomads, means “he runs on the wind,” an appropriate description of its appearance when sprinting through tall houze grass. Modern harkundi generally stand between 1.6 and 1.9 metres at the hips and weigh approximately 200–300 kilograms. Males are usually larger, with the largest individuals exceeding two metres at the hips and approaching 400 kilograms. There is considerable overlap between the sexes, however, and size is not a reliable indicator of sex. Males tend to be darker on average, while both sexes exhibit a range of flaxen, blond, yellow, brown, grey, and white coloration. Pale gold and white morphs are particularly common in desert regions, whereas darker grey and brown individuals are more frequent near forests, coastal areas, and permanent rivers. The harkundi differs considerably from most other phorusrhacids. Whereas many terror birds are robust ambush predators with limited endurance, Torvornis has evolved into a highly cursorial hunter. Its body is supported by a single hoof-like toe, reducing the energy expenditure associated with running, while its bare legs and sparse plumage help limit heat retention. The remaining feathers provide camouflage and some protection from direct sunlight. Its locomotor adaptations allow it to sustain a rapid pace over considerable distances, making it one of the most effective pursuit predators of the prairie. Harkundi can sprint at more than 45 miles per hour and maintain a steady pace of approximately 30 miles per hour for many miles. Although many horses and antelopes can briefly outrun them, these animals generally cannot sustain their fastest speeds indefinitely. The harkundi exploits this difference by maintaining pursuit until its prey begins to tire. It is particularly successful against pronghorns, elasmarians, and other cursorial herbivores. Its ancestors probably specialized in hunting rheas, which were abundant in the region during earlier periods, and this existing specialization on fast, enduring prey provided a foundation for its later success. The expansion of houze grass transformed the ecological landscape in which harkundi evolved. Several smaller terror birds within the genus Torvornis disappeared as the new grassland supported increasing numbers of hyenas and cockatrice. The largest species, however, benefited from the abundance of large ungulates and the reduction of competition in the pursuit-predator niche. It increased in size and became an important predator of the modern prairie. Harkundi are not uniformly social. Some individuals hunt alone, even when prey is abundant, while others form small flocks composed primarily of related animals of the same sex. Female groups are generally territorial, and larger groups tend to defend more extensive territories with greater aggression. Flocks of related hens may contain as many as nine individuals. Males are more frequently solitary, although groups of up to a dozen have been recorded. Mixed-sex groups are uncommon but occur during the breeding season, in areas of exceptional abundance, and during severe dry seasons when cooperation is necessary to capture large prey. Hunting cooperation is particularly important when small prey becomes scarce. Harkundi may work together to corral herds of ungulates or overwhelm a large individual. They also sometimes cooperate with hyenas and cockatrices, although most hunting is conducted independently. Their speed and size allow them to defend kills against many smaller predators, but large cockatrices, hyena mobs, and the scavenger king can still displace them. Harkundi are particularly inclined to defend larger carcasses during the dry season, when opportunities to capture sufficient prey become less frequent. Unlike most terror birds, female harkundi are the primary caretakers of their young. Chicks hatch near the beginning of the wet season and grow exceptionally quickly, approaching adult size within approximately six months. Sexual maturity is generally reached by three years of age. This rapid development allows populations to recover relatively quickly following droughts and other periods of high mortality. Indeed, an abundance of harkundi relative to young uktan is sometimes interpreted by the Dolani as evidence that a region is recovering from severe environmental stress, while a larger proportion of young uktan may indicate a period of greater seasonal stability. These relationships are not reliable enough to serve as definitive ecological indicators, but they are widely recognized in local natural history. Harkundi are among the most dangerous animals encountered by riders. They can outpace a horse carrying a rider, even when an unburdened horse would be faster. Rather than necessarily attacking the horse, they often attempt to seize the rider directly. Some attacks appear to be triggered by the instinct to chase rather than hunger, and individuals have been known to dismount riders without subsequently consuming them. The species epithet hippeiphoneus is sometimes interpreted as meaning “horse eater,” but its original description derives from the Greek word for cavalry and refers to the numerous reports of riders killed by these birds. The harkundi's ecological influence extends beyond its own kills. It is frequently found in the spaces between uktan territories, where it exploits prey and opportunities that the larger megaraptorans do not consistently pursue. This has led to a practical tradition among Dolani nomads: when camping in unfamiliar territory, they often prefer to establish themselves within the marked range of an adult uktan rather than in the open ground between territories. Adult uktan generally concentrate on familiar prey and the protection of their young, whereas harkundi are more likely to investigate unfamiliar opportunities. Consequently, the territory of an apex predator can sometimes provide a more predictable environment than the supposedly open land between them.

The taro, known as the uktaro among the Shu who encounter it most frequently, is a common and successful megaraptoran vassal predator. It is one of the smallest megaraptorans in Kaimere, comparable in mass to the Seridic alar, although it is generally taller and more elongated. Its survival is particularly significant because it represents a lineage that persisted through the extinction of much larger relatives during the Anchored Period. The taro lived alongside Hippodrakon laelaps throughout much of the Anchored Period. While its giant relatives eventually disappeared, the smaller taro survived, probably because of its greater ecological flexibility. Smaller generalist predators are often more resilient during periods of environmental upheaval, particularly when large prey becomes scarce or habitat structure changes. It is also possible that another Hippodrakon species entered the population from Kairul during this period, contributing to the genetic diversity of modern taro. The considerable variation in body size and coloration may reflect this mixed ancestry, although the precise evolutionary history remains uncertain. Modern taro exhibit brown, beige, grey, and gold coloration. During the mating season, individuals develop warmer hues across the face, throat, hands, feet, and tail, producing red-to-yellow flushes that are much less conspicuous during the remainder of the year. Their appearance is unusually variable for a megaraptoran, and it is possible that some characteristics of the extinct giant relatives survive in the modern species. Taro are among the most social megaraptorans. Packs may contain as many as seven related adult females, several males that are usually brothers or cousins, and their offspring. These groups are particularly common around permanent water sources, where they can defend a territory and exploit the concentrated herbivore populations that gather during the dry season. Although they live in groups, taro usually hunt alone or in pairs. Cooperative hunting becomes more common when conditions deteriorate and large prey becomes necessary to sustain the pack. Their hunting anatomy differs from that of the agnotorn megaraptorans. Taro possess serrated teeth that produce deep, tearing wounds, while their powerful arms and claws supplement the bite by striking, buffeting, and rending prey. Agnotorn megaraptorans generally possess smoother gripping teeth and more flexible arms that allow them to restrain prey and deliver precise attacks with their claws. The taro's approach is less precise and considerably bloodier, relying on repeated biting and powerful claw strikes to weaken its target. Solitary taro generally hunt animals weighing less than one ton, although individuals may pursue prey approaching twice their own mass. Pairs and larger groups can attack substantially larger animals. Four individuals working together have been recorded bringing down a giant camel weighing approximately three or four tonnes, which represents the upper end of the species' usual prey range. The taro is also among the fastest megaraptorans. It can sprint at approximately 40 miles per hour, but its most important advantage is endurance. It can maintain a steady pace behind faster ungulates until they become exhausted, making it particularly effective against horses, camels, and other cursorial herbivores. Its maneuverability is comparatively poor, so it is less effective at sudden changes of direction than some smaller predators. Its hunting strategy instead relies on sustained pursuit and the ability to maintain pressure on fleeing prey. Taro are most common on the Houze Prairie and in the northern forests of Ni'Khar. Their distribution is strongly influenced by competition with uktan and harkundi. South of the equator, taro are predominantly nocturnal, reducing direct interaction with the fully diurnal uktan and limiting competition with harkundi. North of the equator, where harkundi are absent or less influential, taro may grow larger and hunt during the day. These northern populations are sometimes specifically called uktaro, meaning “king taro,” reflecting their greater size and reduced competition. The relationship between taro and uktan is particularly unusual. The uktan's prolonged parental care leaves the juvenile predator niche relatively open, allowing taro to remain an important predator despite the presence of enormous megaraptorans. Taro reach full size in approximately eight to ten years, whereas uktan grow much more rapidly. The rapid development of harkundi also places considerable pressure on taro populations in the southern prairie, particularly where the two species compete for the same prey. The relationship between taro and uktan is not simply one of competition. Uktan are known to react strongly to the scent and presence of taro, and taro living near them are consequently more likely to become nocturnal. In northern regions, where harkundi do not exert the same pressure, taro can grow larger and occupy a more dominant position among local predators. In the Known World, however, the exceptionally high density of competing fauna encourages greater niche partitioning between species and even between neighboring taro populations. Northern populations sometimes specialize in different prey to reduce competition with one another, while southern populations more commonly partition resources between species.

Apex Predator of the Prairie

The apex predator community of the Houze Prairie is dominated by the uktan, an enormous megaraptoran theropod that occupies the highest terrestrial predatory position across the open grasslands of Ni'Khar and parts of Kairul. Its size, endurance, intelligence, and social organization distinguish it from the other predators of the prairie. Unlike the officers and vassals, which are generally limited to particular prey sizes or hunting strategies, adult uktan can kill nearly any terrestrial animal in their environment. Their presence also shapes the behavior and distribution of other predators, making them the principal ecological force governing the open grasslands. The uktan, whose name means "king of the prairie" in the language of the Dolani nomads, is an enormous cursorial megaraptoran. Although related to the zentaur, it has evolved a distinct body plan adapted to sustained overland travel. Its torso is nearly as wide as it is tall, giving it a robust appearance, but extensive pneumaticity throughout the skeleton and air sacs extending from the lungs make it exceptionally light for its dimensions. A similarly sized uktan may weigh as much as two tonnes less than a zentaur or tyrannosaur. This lightweight construction gives the animal extraordinary locomotor efficiency. Uktan can maintain a steady jog for hours when pursuing prey, making them among the most effective endurance predators in Kaimere. They are capable of running down animals that are substantially faster than themselves, including horses, pronghorns, and camels. A horse may briefly reach speeds far beyond those of an adult uktan, but it cannot maintain a sprint indefinitely. The uktan instead follows at a steady pace, exploiting its respiratory efficiency until the prey becomes exhausted. The species is particularly successful in the Houze Prairie because its principal prey are cursorial ungulates. Horses, pronghorns, camels, and other large grazers are abundant, and many are adapted for rapid travel across open terrain. Uktan do not need to be the fastest animals in the ecosystem; they need only to maintain a pace that their prey cannot sustain. Ambush can be effective when cover permits, but prolonged pursuit is the principal hunting strategy. Adult uktan can bring down animals within their own size class, although most prey consists of smaller herbivores. Their hunting behavior changes with age and social circumstances. Adults may concentrate on familiar prey species within their territory, while juveniles and adolescents exploit smaller or more opportunistic targets. Adolescent uktan frequently participate in hunts by corralling prey toward their parents and older siblings, functioning much like hunting dogs. This cooperation allows the family to capture animals that might otherwise escape an adult hunter acting alone. Uktan are highly intelligent and socially complex. Adults are generally monogamous and are believed to mate for life. Pairs establish and defend territories, laying between three and eight eggs annually depending on nutritional conditions. Their young are raised within a family structure that may persist for more than a decade. During the first year, parents provide processed food and protection. Thereafter, juveniles begin to hunt and scavenge independently, although they remain under the protection of adults and older siblings. The first years of life are particularly dangerous. Hatchlings are vulnerable to small predators, including honey badgers, hyenas, and harkundi. A predator that would be no threat to an adult uktan may kill a young animal. As juveniles grow, however, they become increasingly capable of defending themselves and their siblings. By approximately four years of age, they can run and hunt small prey, although they remain dependent on their family for protection and much of their food. At around nine or ten years, they are large and fast enough to participate meaningfully in family hunts, helping to corral prey toward adults. By this age, juvenile uktan are also generally safe from most of the smaller predators of the prairie. Their growth accelerates dramatically around ten years of age, with individuals potentially gaining approximately a tonne annually during this period. Females generally reach adult size by thirteen or fourteen years, measuring approximately 35 feet in length and weighing four to five tonnes. Males continue growing throughout their lives, although their growth rate decreases with age. They may live for approximately 35–40 years. Sexual dimorphism becomes pronounced in adulthood. Males develop a large keratinous boss extending along the snout and branching into three yellow hornlets above each brow. Additional yellow keratinous projections occur near the jugal region, while the skin takes on a yellow hue. Males also develop a prominent reddish-brown mane and tail plumage, with thicker body plumage than females. Adult females generally retain grey skin, lack the prominent cranial boss, and possess smaller hornlets and more subdued plumage. Despite their strong familial bonds, uktan are highly territorial. They can be cannibalistic and may attack, kill, and consume unrelated individuals that enter their territories. Their social intelligence is demonstrated by intergenerational learning, including the transmission of specialized hunting techniques and nest construction methods. They also possess a wide vocal range, including infrasonic calls used for long-distance communication. Like elephants, they appear to use specific calls to address individual members of their social group, effectively assigning names to one another. The species' territorial behavior has considerable consequences for the wider carnivore community. Adult uktan are generally intolerant of other large megaraptorans, and their scent and visual detection of taro appears to provoke particularly strong responses. Smaller predators may therefore adjust their activity patterns to avoid direct contact. Taro become more nocturnal in areas with substantial uktan activity, while harkundi frequently concentrate in the spaces between uktan territories. This creates a landscape in which the apex predator indirectly organizes the distribution and behavior of the vassal community. The ecological dominance of uktan should not be interpreted as independence from the rest of the ecosystem. Like all apex predators, their populations depend upon the productivity of the trophic levels below them. The enormous food requirements of adults and their extended parental care make successful reproduction dependent on sustained prey availability. A healthy population of uktan therefore reflects the productivity of the prairie, rather than being solely responsible for it. Their dominance is ultimately conditional upon the abundance of grazing mammals and the stability of the seasonal systems that support them.
